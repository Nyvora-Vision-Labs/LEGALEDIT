\begin{abstract}
\noindent
An automatic metric for \emph{legal} meaning preservation is supposed to answer
a practical question: does this simplified clause still say what the original
said? We ask what evidence would justify believing such an answer, and argue
that the evidence currently collected cannot. The two automated checks in
standard use, which require that an identical pair score at the top of the
range and an unrelated pair at the bottom, move lexical overlap and legal force
in the same direction at the same time, so that any monotone function of token
overlap satisfies both; and a correlation with averaged human ratings is
informative only to the extent that the average is reliable and the competing
metrics were scored on a common scale. We propose a four-part validation protocol that closes each
gap: a measured human ceiling, calibration of every candidate to the human scale
under identical supervision, a trivial surface-feature control, and a
\emph{dissociation} diagnostic that holds surface form fixed and moves the law.
The diagnostic is the central instrument and we release it as
\textsc{LegalEdit}: \numedits\ minimal perturbations of Quebec statutory French
(\emph{doit}\,/\,\emph{peut}, \emph{et}\,/\,\emph{ou},
\emph{assur\'e}\,/\,\emph{assureur}, dropped negations, amounts multiplied by
ten) that reverse legal force while preserving \meanjaccard\ of the tokens.
Instantiating the protocol on \textsc{FrJudge} and \textsc{JudgeBERT}
\citep{beauchemin2025judgebert}, currently the only corpus and supervised metric
for this construct, changes four conclusions. The corpus is more reliable than
its published coefficient suggests, because a \emph{nominal} $\alpha$ was
applied to a ten-point \emph{ordinal} scale; the ordinal value is $\alphaOrd$.
The reported headline correlation sits \emph{above} the human ceiling: one
annotator predicts the consensus of the other four at $r=\ceilR$ and the
best-agreeing pair reaches $r=\bestPairR$, against $r=0.74$ for the metric.
Under identical supervision a ridge regression over ten \emph{surface} features
reaches $r=\surfR$, level with that ceiling and above every neural baseline.
And \textsc{LegalEdit} splits the field in two: every similarity-based scorer
spends only $\simMarginLo$--$\bertMargin$ of its identical-to-unrelated range on
an edit that reverses the law, and $\partyMargin$ on the edits that swap
\emph{l'assur\'e} for \emph{l'assureur}, while bidirectional NLI entailment
spends $\maxMargin$ and ranks $93\%$ of items correctly. Crucially the two
orderings come apart: the best-correlating signal on the corpus is the worst on
the diagnostic, so a metric chosen by correlation alone is chosen wrongly.
Retraining under an evaluation that never scores a model on its own
augmentation reverses a further result: the augmentation reported to raise $r$
from $0.74$ to $0.97$ instead costs more than half of it, on every seed.
Distributional and quantile objectives, by contrast, buy stability and risk
asymmetry rather than accuracy. Code, corrected splits, and the diagnostic are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Rewriting a legal text for a lay reader has an unusual failure mode. When a news
summary drops a qualifier the reader is mildly misinformed; when an insurance
clause drops a qualifier the reader may be uninsured. The 2024 decision in
\emph{Moffatt v.\ Air Canada}, which held an airline liable for its chatbot's
misstatement of its own bereavement-fare policy, established that the gap
between what a document says and what an automated system says it says is
legally cognisable and not merely academic.

If simplified legal text is going to be produced automatically, something has to
check it, and the something will in practice be an automatic metric. That places
an unusually heavy burden of proof on the metric. It is not enough that its
scores rise and fall roughly with human ratings on a corpus of naturally
occurring simplifications. What a practitioner needs to know is narrower and
harder: does the score move when the legal effect of the clause moves, and does
it stay put when the wording changes without legal effect? Those two properties
are what make a number safe to act on, and neither is measured by a correlation
coefficient.

This paper is about how to measure them. Our starting observation is that the
validation apparatus the field has converged on cannot, even in principle,
distinguish a metric that reads law from a metric that counts shared words. Two
automated checks are in current use \citep{beauchemin2023meaningbert,
beauchemin2025judgebert}: a sentence paired with itself must receive the maximum
score, and a sentence paired with an unrelated one must receive the minimum.
Both are necessary and both are easy to state. But moving from an identical pair
to an unrelated pair changes lexical overlap and legal meaning \emph{together},
in the same direction, at the same time. Any monotone function of token overlap
therefore satisfies both checks by construction, which is exactly why the
embedding metrics in the literature already pass them. The checks are a test of
calibration at two endpoints, not a test of what the metric is reading.

The remedy is a \emph{dissociation}: an evaluation item on which the two
confounded variables are pulled apart, so that legal force changes while surface
form does not. Consider a clause in which \emph{doit} (must) is replaced by
\emph{peut} (may). Ninety-odd percent of the tokens are untouched, the sentence
remains fluent and statutory in register, and an obligation has become a
permission. A metric that measures legal meaning must drop sharply; a metric
that measures overlap cannot. This construction is the core methodological
contribution of the paper, and we build \textsc{LegalEdit}, a set of \numedits\
such perturbations over Quebec statutory French, to carry it out.

Around that instrument we assemble three further requirements, each of which
addresses a way that a correlation number can be read for more than it is worth.
A correlation should be interpreted against a \emph{measured} human ceiling,
which is free to compute whenever a corpus ships several ratings per item.
Competing metrics should be compared under \emph{identical supervision}, since
an unsupervised similarity score and a regressor fitted to the label are not
otherwise on the same scale. And every comparison should include a
\emph{trivial-feature control}, because the surface statistics of a
simplification, above all the degree to which it is shorter, carry more of the
human label than is usually assumed. Together with the diagnostic these form a
four-part protocol, set out in \S\ref{sec:protocol}.

To find out whether the protocol changes anything, we instantiate it on the only
corpus and metric that currently exist for this construct.
\citet{beauchemin2025judgebert} argue, convincingly in our view, that
\emph{legal} meaning preservation is not general meaning preservation: in
ordinary French \emph{automobile} and \emph{v\'ehicule} are near-synonyms, but
under Quebec's Highway Safety Code an automobile is moved by mechanical force
and a vehicle by mechanical \emph{or human} force, so every bicycle is a
counterexample. They contribute \textsc{FrJudge}, 297 clause/simplification
pairs from Quebec property-insurance forms rated by five law students, and
\textsc{JudgeBERT}, a CamemBERTv2 regressor reported at $r=0.74$ against the
mean human rating, rising to $0.97$ with data augmentation, against $0.46$ for
the strongest baseline. The construct and the corpus are the right foundation,
and the work below is possible at all only because the raw annotations were
released. Every component of the protocol changes a conclusion.

\paragraph{The gold standard is weaker, and stronger, than it appears.}
Reproducing the inter-annotator statistics exactly (our mean pairwise percent
agreement matches the published $25.96$ to two decimals), we find that the
reported $\alpha=0.10$ applies a nominal coefficient to an ordinal scale,
counting a $9$-versus-$10$ disagreement as fully as a $1$-versus-$10$
disagreement. The coefficient appropriate to the design is $\alphaOrd$
(\S\ref{sec:agreement}). The disagreement that remains, however, is structural
rather than random: the five annotators split into a lenient group (means
$\lenientMean$) and a strict group ($\strictMean$), and on \pctSpreadSix\ of
pairs their ratings span six points or more. The mean of five such ratings is a
quantity no annotator asserted.

\paragraph{The reported correlation exceeds the ceiling it should be read against.}
Because the corpus ships five ratings per item, the ceiling is measurable: a
held-out annotator predicts the mean of the other four at $r=\ceilR$
(RMSE $\ceilRmse$), and the best-agreeing \emph{pair} of annotators reaches
$r=\bestPairR$ (\S\ref{sec:ceiling}). A model reported at $r=0.74$ is therefore
not better than a human at judging legal meaning; it is better than any human at
predicting a five-rater average, which is a different and much easier task, and
one that rewards predicting the middle of a bimodal distribution. We show how to
report the two claims separately.

\paragraph{Surface features do most of the work.}
Unsupervised similarity metrics are conventionally scored against a $1$--$10$
Likert label without ever being fitted to that scale, so their RMSE measures the
absence of supervision rather than the presence of error. After isotonic
calibration on the training split the ordering changes substantially, and a
ridge regression over ten surface features (word-count difference, token
overlap, numeral overlap) reaches $r=\surfR$, above every neural baseline and
level with the human ceiling (\S\ref{sec:baselines}). Legal meaning
preservation, as this corpus operationalises it, is to a large extent length
preservation.

\paragraph{The diagnostic splits the field in two.}
Under \textsc{LegalEdit} the similarity metrics collapse: BERTScore places a
sentence that says the opposite of the original at $0.982$ where the original
itself sits at $1.000$. Bidirectional NLI entailment retains most of its
discrimination (\S\ref{sec:results}). The diagnostic is therefore not an
impossible bar, but one that an existing family clears and another does not.
Awkwardly for current practice, the family that clears it is precisely the
family that the identical-pair check would have disqualified.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i) An argument that the sanity checks in current use are jointly satisfiable by
lexical overlap and therefore cannot certify a legal metric, and a four-part
validation protocol that repairs this; (ii) \textsc{LegalEdit}, a \numedits-item
dissociation diagnostic defined by legal effect rather than by linguistic
category, on which every similarity-based metric fails, bidirectional entailment
does not, and corpus correlation and legal sensitivity come apart; (iii) the
first human-ceiling estimate for legal meaning preservation, together with a
recomputation of the \textsc{FrJudge} annotation statistics from the raw export
and a correction to its reliability coefficient; (iv) a calibrated baseline
comparison including trivial surface features, showing how much reported
headroom is attributable to supervision and to length; (v) a retraining study
showing that identical/unrelated augmentation, once held out of the evaluation
set, costs more than half the reported correlation, and that disagreement-aware
and risk-asymmetric objectives buy stability and calibration rather than
accuracy; and (vi) a reproducibility and privacy review of the public artefacts.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Evaluating text simplification.}
Simplification quality is conventionally decomposed into fluency, simplicity and
meaning preservation, and the metrics borrowed from translation and
summarisation transfer poorly to the third. \citet{sulem2018bleu} show BLEU
\citep{papineni2002bleu} does not correlate with meaning preservation once
sentence splitting is involved, the operation most characteristic of legal
simplification \citep{garimella2022text}. SARI
\citep{xu-etal-2016-optimizing} targets simplicity rather than adequacy, and the
reference corpora it is computed against \citep{alvamanchego2020asset} are
general-domain. Embedding metrics \citep{zhang2019bertscore,reimers2019sbert}
capture semantic variability better but, as \citet{cripwell2024document}
document, score consistently high across \emph{all} alterations, penalising even
substantial meaning changes too little; \citet{kew2023bless} reach a similar
conclusion when benchmarking LLM simplifiers. Trained metrics such as LENS
\citep{maddela2023lens} and MeaningBERT \citep{beauchemin2023meaningbert}
improve correlation on the corpora they are fitted to.
\citet{agrawal2024meaning} argue for grounding meaning preservation in reading
comprehension rather than similarity, and \citet{trienes2024infolossqa}
characterise simplification errors as recoverable \emph{information loss}, a
framing close to the omission category that dominates legal error taxonomies.

\paragraph{French and legal resources.}
French simplification corpora are few and none are legal: Alector, CLEAR
\citep{grabar2018clear} and WikiLarge-FR \citep{cardon2020wikilargefr} cover
literacy, medical and encyclopaedic text respectively. Legal NLP resources are
mostly English and mostly not about simplification: CUAD
\citep{hendrycks2021cuad} annotates contract clauses for extraction, and
\citet{garneau2022criminal} work on French criminal-law text without
simplification annotations. \citet{hagan2023good} proposes criteria a legal AI
system should be benchmarked against, two of which (that a response covers
details and exceptions, and that it does not misrepresent the substantive law)
form the basis of the construct \citet{beauchemin2025judgebert} operationalise
in \textsc{FrJudge}, the corpus and metric we take as our case study. To our
knowledge it remains the only annotated resource for legal meaning preservation
in any language.

\paragraph{Metric robustness and challenge sets.}
Our diagnostic follows a line of work showing that correlation on naturally
occurring outputs hides brittleness on targeted perturbations.
\citet{sai2021perturbation} build perturbation checklists for NLG metrics;
\citet{anschutz2023negation} show metrics systematically miss negation;
\citet{chen2023menli} demonstrate that similarity-based metrics are not robust
to meaning-changing edits and that NLI-based metrics repair much of the gap.
\textsc{LegalEdit} adapts the method to a domain where the perturbations are not
linguistic curiosities but the exact operations that decide coverage disputes,
and where the perturbation inventory is therefore organised by legal effect
rather than by linguistic category. To our knowledge no prior challenge set
targets legal force.

\paragraph{Factual consistency and entailment.}
Adequacy in summarisation is measured with entailment- and QA-based methods:
SummaC \citep{laban2022summac}, AlignScore \citep{zha2023alignscore},
QAFactEval \citep{fabbri2022qafacteval}, QuestEval \citep{rebuffel2021questeval},
building on earlier consistency work \citep{laban2020summary,hayes2007answering}.
These are English-only, which is why they are absent from the French literature;
we construct a French analogue by running a multilingual NLI model
\citep{laurer2024nli,conneau2020xlmr} in both directions, so that failure of
$o \Rightarrow s$ signals hallucination and failure of $s \Rightarrow o$ signals
omission. The two directions map onto the two dominant categories of the legal
error taxonomy, which is why we treat this family as the strongest available
starting point rather than as one baseline among many.

\paragraph{Annotator disagreement.}
The assumption that a single gold label exists has been extensively challenged
\citep{pavlick2019inherent,plank2022problem}. \citet{uma2021learning} survey
methods that learn from the label distribution rather than its mode, and
\citet{peterson2019human} show soft labels improve robustness. Legal
interpretation is a paradigm case: disagreement between competent lawyers is the
ordinary condition of the field, not annotator error. We accordingly treat the
five ratings per item as a distribution to be modelled (\S\ref{sec:objectives})
rather than averaged.

\paragraph{Meta-evaluation methodology.}
\citet{deutsch2021statistical} show correlation estimates for generation metrics
carry wide confidence intervals and that apparent metric differences frequently
fail significance testing; \citet{graham2014williams} give the appropriate test
for dependent correlations; \citet{card2020power} show NLP comparisons are
routinely underpowered. Our protocol applies all three as a matter of course.

\section{What Would Validate a Legal Meaning Metric?}
\label{sec:protocol}

Before running any experiment we set out what we think the evidence should
consist of. The four requirements below are stated for an arbitrary scorer
$m(o,s) \in \mathbb{R}$ taking an original clause $o$ and a candidate
simplification $s$; none of them presuppose a particular architecture, and all
four are cheap enough that we see no reason for a metric paper to omit them.

\subsection{Requirement 1: a measured ceiling}

A correlation with human judgment means nothing until one knows what correlation
a human achieves. Where a corpus carries $k \geq 3$ ratings per item, this is
free: hold out one rater, correlate their ratings against the mean of the
remaining $k-1$, and average over the held-out position. Two bounds should be
reported, because they answer different questions. The leave-one-annotator-out
correlation answers \emph{is the metric as good as an expert?} The
Spearman--Brown reliability of the $k$-rater mean, and its square root, answer
\emph{is the metric as good as the aggregate label allows?} A metric can sit
above the first and below the second without being superhuman at anything,
because predicting an average is easier than being one of the raters averaged:
aggregation cancels idiosyncratic variance that no individual can cancel for
themselves. Reporting one bound and not the other invites exactly the conflation
we document in \S\ref{sec:ceiling}.

\subsection{Requirement 2: identical supervision}

Comparisons in this literature routinely place an unsupervised cosine similarity
and a regressor fine-tuned on the label in the same table, normalise the former
by decimal scaling into the label range, and report RMSE for both. Decimal
scaling is not calibration: it fixes the range but neither the location nor the
shape of the distribution. A cosine similarity that occupies $[0.7,1.0]$ maps to
high scores everywhere and records a large RMSE however well it ranks items. The
resulting column measures access to supervision, and the companion statistic
usually reported alongside it, namely the fraction of predictions above the
human label, follows mechanically from the same asymmetry rather than from any
difference in judgment.

The repair is to fit a monotone map from each metric's raw score to the human
scale on the training split only, and to evaluate on the held-out split, so that
every candidate is compared under the same supervision. We use isotonic
regression \citep{zadrozny2002transforming}, which cannot improve a metric's
ranking of items because it is monotone; it removes the scale artefact and
nothing else. Rank statistics ($\rho$) and Pearson $r$ are unaffected for the
unsupervised metrics, which is a useful check that the map is doing only what it
is supposed to.

\subsection{Requirement 3: a trivial-feature control}

Simplification changes surface statistics in systematic ways, and the most
systematic of them is length: LLM simplifications are usually shorter, and the
dominant annotated error category in legal simplification is omission. A rubric
that deducts score once per identified error will therefore produce a label
partly predictable from how many words disappeared, with no representation of
legal force involved. Any comparison that omits a length control will attribute
to a neural metric what belongs to that confound. We therefore require a
supervised baseline over surface features alone (word counts, their difference,
token overlap and numeral overlap), fitted under exactly the protocol of
Requirement 2 and reported in the same table as the neural metrics.

\subsection{Requirement 4: dissociation}

The three requirements above discipline the corpus-correlation evidence. They do
not, and cannot, establish what a metric is reading, because on naturally
occurring simplification pairs surface form and legal force vary together. This
is the same confound that makes the identical/unrelated checks uninformative,
and it needs the same remedy: an evaluation set on which the two are pulled
apart by construction.

Formally, let $s'$ be a perturbation of $s$ with token Jaccard
$J(s,s') \approx 1$ and legal force $L(s') \neq L(s)$, and let $u$ be an
unrelated sentence of the same register. We report the \emph{margin fraction}
\[
\text{margin} \;=\; \frac{\overline{m(s,s)} - \overline{m(s,s')}}
                          {\overline{m(s,s)} - \overline{m(s,u)}},
\]
the share of the metric's own identical-to-unrelated dynamic range that it
spends on a legally decisive edit. The normalisation matters: it makes the
statistic comparable across metrics with incomparable raw scales, and it charges
each metric against the range it actually has rather than against a nominal
$[0,1]$. A metric that treats the flipped sentence as it treats an unrelated one
scores $1$; a pure overlap function scores near $0$.

Two properties of this statistic are worth stating in advance. It is not the
rate at which a metric ranks $(s,s)$ above $(s,s')$, and the rate is much the
weaker quantity: a metric can rank correctly on every item while moving by a
thousandth of its range, which is a ranking that carries no decision. And the
margin is not redundant with corpus correlation. Whether the two agree is an
empirical question, and \S\ref{sec:results} reports that on the corpus at hand
they do not.

\subsection{Instantiation}

The remainder of the paper carries out the protocol. \S\ref{sec:legaledit}
constructs the diagnostic; \S\ref{sec:corpus} and \S\ref{sec:ceiling} supply the
corpus reconstruction and the ceiling; \S\ref{sec:baselines} the calibrated
comparison and the trivial control; \S\ref{sec:results} the diagnostic results.
\S\S\ref{sec:objectives}--\ref{sec:general} then ask what the protocol implies
for training a metric, rather than only for auditing one.

\section{\textsc{LegalEdit}: Constructing the Dissociation}
\label{sec:legaledit}

\subsection{Source text and edit inventory}

The perturbations need source sentences that are statutory in register,
plentiful, and regular enough in phrasing that a rule can apply cleanly. We draw
them from two Quebec statutes held out from any corpus used elsewhere in the
paper, the Automobile Insurance Act and the Highway Safety Code, and apply a
single minimal edit that changes legal force while leaving the sentence
otherwise intact. The inventory is organised by legal effect rather than by
linguistic category, which is the respect in which it departs from general-domain
perturbation suites:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Modality}: \emph{doit} $\leftrightarrow$ \emph{peut}; obligation
      becomes permission.
\item \textbf{Party}: \emph{l'assur\'e} $\leftrightarrow$ \emph{l'assureur};
      the duty changes hands.
\item \textbf{Polarity}: dropping \emph{ne \dots pas}; \emph{couvre}
      $\leftrightarrow$ \emph{ne couvre pas}; \emph{exclut} $\leftrightarrow$
      \emph{inclut}.
\item \textbf{Quantum}: the first monetary or temporal amount multiplied by ten
      (\emph{12 mois} $\rightarrow$ \emph{120 mois}).
\item \textbf{Scope}: \emph{tous les} $\rightarrow$ \emph{certains};
      \emph{sauf si} $\rightarrow$ \emph{si}.
\item \textbf{Temporal}: \emph{avant} $\leftrightarrow$ \emph{apr\`es}.
\item \textbf{Class} (tier 2): \emph{automobile} $\leftrightarrow$
      \emph{v\'ehicule}, the defining example of the construct; and \emph{et}
      $\leftrightarrow$ \emph{ou}, which converts conjunctive conditions to
      disjunctive ones.
\end{itemize}

We separate a \emph{tier 1} set, in which the edit unambiguously changes who is
bound, what is covered, or how much, from a \emph{tier 2} set of scope and class
shifts, and report both.

\subsection{Keeping the edit invisible to a fluency detector}

A diagnostic of this kind fails if a metric can find the edited item by noticing
that it reads badly. Perturbations that introduce a grammatical artefact are
therefore filtered by a conservative French well-formedness check covering
failed elision (\emph{le automobile}), missing contraction (\emph{de le}),
doubled negation, and determiner--noun gender disagreement. The check flags none
of the $400$ unmodified source sentences, so it removes only genuine artefacts
rather than trimming the set toward any particular rule.

The result is \numedits\ pairs (\numeditsTierOne\ tier 1) with mean token
Jaccard \meanjaccard\ against their source, none below \minjaccard.
Table~\ref{tab:examples} gives one example per rule.

\subsection{Administering the diagnostic}

For each source sentence $s$ with perturbation $s'$ we score three pairs:
$(s,s)$, $(s,s')$, and $(s,u)$ for an unrelated sentence $u$ drawn from the same
statutes. The metric's scores are min--max normalised per metric so that the
identical and unrelated probes anchor its range, and the margin fraction of
\S\ref{sec:protocol} is computed from the three means. Because the harness takes
an arbitrary scoring function $m$, the same procedure applies unchanged to
untrained metrics, to fine-tuned regressors, and to a prompted LLM judge.

\section{Case Study: The \textsc{FrJudge} Corpus}
\label{sec:corpus}

\textsc{FrJudge} contains 297 pairs drawn from two Quebec insurance forms (one
BAC home-insurance form, the AMF automobile forms), each simplified by
\texttt{gpt-4-turbo-2024-04-09} with a zero-shot prompt and rated by five law
students on legal meaning ($1$--$10$), simplicity (4 levels), and an 18-class
legal characterization.

Two files are public. \texttt{FrJUDGE.jsonl} contains 1{,}485 rows
($297\times5$) but \emph{drops annotator identity}, which makes annotator-level
analysis, and with it Requirement 1, impossible for a downstream user.
The raw Prodigy export retains it in \texttt{\_annotator\_id}. We rebuild the
corpus from that export, recovering the five annotators (pseudonymised A--E
here; see \S\ref{sec:ethics}), the source document of each clause, and
per-annotation timestamps. We verify the reconstruction against the published
statistics: our mean pairwise percent agreement is $25.96\%$, matching the
published value exactly, and our recovered per-annotator means ($\annotMeans$)
match the published figure to within rounding. Everything downstream rests on
this reconstruction, so we state the check before using it.

\subsection{The reliability coefficient}
\label{sec:agreement}

Table~\ref{tab:agreement} resolves a discrepancy. Our nominal $\alpha$ for legal
meaning is $0.104$, reproducing the published $0.10$; our nominal $\alpha$ for
simplicity ($0.183$) and characterization ($0.552$) likewise reproduce the
published $0.18$ and $0.55$. But legal meaning is measured on an ordered
ten-point scale, and nominal $\alpha$ counts a one-point disagreement as fully
as a nine-point one. With the ordinal coefficient appropriate to the design
\citep{krippendorff2004content,artstein2008inter} the value is $\alphaOrd$.

This matters in both directions. The corpus is more reliable than the published
coefficient makes it look, which is a point in its favour that a reader would
otherwise miss. But $\alphaOrd$ still indicates only fair agreement, and the
disagreement that remains is not noise.

\subsection{The structure of disagreement}

The five annotators separate into two stable populations
(Figure~\ref{fig:disagree}a): B, C and D average $\lenientMean$ and A and E
average $\strictMean$, a gap of over three points on a ten-point scale. Ratings
are strongly bimodal (the two modal values are $10$ and $1$), the median
across-annotator range on a single pair is $\medianRange$ points, and
\pctSpreadSix\ of pairs span at least six points (Figure~\ref{fig:disagree}b).
Averaging produces a target with a standard deviation of only $\labelSd$,
substantially narrower than the underlying ratings, which is precisely the
condition under which a regressor that predicts the middle scores well. The
observation is not incidental to the ceiling that follows; it is the reason the
ceiling and the aggregate-reliability bound differ so much.

\section{The Human Ceiling}
\label{sec:ceiling}

Requirement 1 is directly executable here, because every item carries five
ratings. For each annotator we correlate their ratings against the mean of the
other four (Table~\ref{tab:ceiling}). The mean leave-one-annotator-out
correlation is $\ceilR$ (RMSE $\ceilRmse$); the best-agreeing pair of annotators
reaches $r=\bestPairR$ and the mean pairwise correlation is $\meanPairR$.

\begin{table}[t]\centering\small

\caption{Leave-one-annotator-out ceiling on \textsc{FrJudge} ($n=297$).
A single expert predicts the consensus of the other four at $r=\ceilR$.
\textsc{JudgeBERT} is reported at $r=0.74$.}
\label{tab:ceiling}
\end{table}

\paragraph{How to read a metric that beats it.}
A model exceeding $\ceilR$ is not thereby superhuman. Predicting the \emph{mean}
of five raters is easier than being one of them, for the reason given in
\S\ref{sec:protocol}. The appropriate upper bound for predicting the observed
mean is set by the reliability of that mean, which by Spearman--Brown from the
mean pairwise correlation is $\sbRel$; a metric can in principle approach
$\sqrt{\sbRel}=\sbSqrt$. Reporting both bounds separates two claims that a
single correlation number runs together: \emph{as good as an expert}
($r \approx \ceilR$) and \emph{as good as the aggregate allows}
($r \approx \sbSqrt$). A reported $0.74$ sits comfortably between them, which is
the whole point: it is evidence for the second claim and evidence against
nothing at all with respect to the first. We recommend that any future
legal-meaning metric report against both.

\section{Metrics Under Identical Supervision}
\label{sec:baselines}

\subsection{The comparison set}

Requirement 2 asks for a comparison in which every candidate sees the same
supervision, and Requirement 3 for a trivial control inside it. We use
French-capable metrics throughout, since scoring English-only metrics on French
text confounds language coverage with metric quality: BERTScore
\citep{zhang2019bertscore} over CamemBERTv2 \citep{antoun2024camembert2} and
FlauBERT \citep{le2020flaubert}, both F1 and recall-only, since recall directly
targets omission; sentence-embedding cosine with LaBSE \citep{feng2022labse},
Sentence-CamemBERT, multilingual mpnet, and multilingual E5; and bidirectional
NLI entailment with mDeBERTa-XNLI \citep{laurer2024nli}. To these we add the
trivial surface features and a supervised ridge regression over ten of them.
Each metric's raw score is mapped to the human scale by an isotonic regression
fitted on the training split alone and evaluated on the held-out test split.

\subsection{What identical supervision changes}

Table~\ref{tab:baselines} reports the result, and three things in it are worth
separating. First, calibration collapses most of the RMSE gap that uncalibrated
comparisons record: the metrics were not as far from human judgment as such
tables suggest, they were merely on a different scale. Second, the strongest
single unsupervised signal is not a semantic one. Third, a ridge regression over
word counts and token overlap reaches $r=\surfR$, which is at the human ceiling
of \S\ref{sec:ceiling} and above every neural baseline. Whatever a fine-tuned
legal metric adds beyond that, a comparison without this row does not measure.

We do not read this as evidence that the corpus is trivial. We read it as
evidence about what the \emph{annotation} rewards. Under the published rubric,
score is reduced once per identified error, and the most frequent error type in
LLM simplification is omission, which is strongly correlated with becoming
shorter. A metric can therefore score well by detecting deletion, without any
representation of legal force. That hypothesis is exactly what Requirement 4
exists to test, and \S\ref{sec:results} tests it.

\subsection{Statistical adequacy}

The test split holds $\testN$ items. At that size, the smallest correlation
reliably distinguishable from $r=0.46$ at $80\%$ power is $\mindetect$; a
reported gap between $0.74$ and $0.46$ is therefore at the edge of
detectability, and finer comparisons on a split this size are not resolvable at
all. We report bootstrap confidence intervals throughout, following
\citet{deutsch2021statistical}, and use the Williams test for dependent
correlations \citep{graham2014williams} when comparing metrics against the same
human scores.

Table~\ref{tab:significance} applies that test to the comparison the previous
subsection turns on. Every neural metric and the length feature correlate with
the \emph{same} human label on the \emph{same} $297$ items, and with each other,
so the comparison is dependent and a two-sample test on Fisher-transformed
correlations would be anticonservative. Under Williams, the length feature's
advantage is significant against every metric in the comparison set. We stress
what this does and does not show: it establishes that the trivial baseline is
genuinely stronger \emph{on this corpus}, not that the neural metrics reason
less well about law. Distinguishing those two readings is what the diagnostic is
for.

\section{Diagnostic Results: Two Families, Not One}
\label{sec:results}

Table~\ref{tab:challenge} and Figure~\ref{fig:challenge} report the margin over
\nMetricsChallenged\ scorers, and the result is sharper than we expected. The
metrics divide cleanly into two families.

Every \emph{similarity}-based metric fails, and fails almost completely. The
seven embedding and BERTScore variants spend between $\simMarginLo$ and
$\bertMargin$ of their working range on an edit that reverses the law: BERTScore
over CamemBERTv2 places a sentence saying the opposite of the original at
$0.982$ on a scale where the original itself sits at $1.000$ and an unrelated
statute sits at $0.193$. Token Jaccard, at $0.079$, is in the same regime, which
is the point: these metrics are behaving as overlap functions, exactly as the
argument of \S\ref{sec:protocol} says they must. Their \emph{discrimination}
rates are uninformative for the same reason: they rank the identical pair above
the edited one $100\%$ of the time, but by a margin so small that it carries no
decision. This is the concrete case the margin statistic was defined to catch.

Bidirectional NLI does not fail. It spends $\maxMargin$ of its range on the
edit, ranks correctly on $93.3\%$ of items, and places a legally flipped
sentence ($0.265$) nearer the unrelated pair ($0.010$) than the identical one
($0.784$). The two directions individually reach $0.424$ and $0.473$; taking
their minimum, which is what an omission-\emph{or}-hallucination reading of the
construct requires, is what produces the $0.670$. On the property the construct
is about, this family is not merely competitive but in a different class.

\paragraph{Relation to \textsc{MENLI}.}
That entailment survives meaning-changing edits better than similarity is not a
new claim: \citet{chen2023menli} establish it for general-domain NLG metrics,
and our result is in that sense a confirmation rather than a discovery. We think
the confirmation is worth having, since it was not obvious that it would
transfer to a domain where the decisive edits are single function words in
statutory French, nor that the gap would be this large. But we want to be clear
about what is new here and what is not. Three things are. The perturbation
inventory is defined by legal effect rather than by linguistic category, so the
diagnostic speaks to a deployment question (may this metric gate a simplified
clause?) rather than to metric robustness in general. The dissociation between
corpus correlation and legal sensitivity reported below is a property of this
corpus and could not be read off \textsc{MENLI}. And the observation in the next
paragraph, that the sanity check this literature has standardised on would have
excluded the entailment family outright, is specific to how legal-meaning
metrics are currently validated.

\paragraph{The existing checks would have rejected it.}
Note where NLI's identical score sits: $0.784$, not $1.000$. This is not a
normalisation artefact. In raw terms mDeBERTa assigns a mean entailment of
$\nliIdentRaw$ to a sentence paired with \emph{itself}, and clears the $99\%$
bar required by current practice \citep{beauchemin2025judgebert} on only
$\nliIdentPass\%$ of items, against $100\%$ for every BERTScore and embedding
metric. The metric that best tracks legal meaning is therefore the one metric
that would be \emph{disqualified} by the sanity check in use. We take this as
the strongest available argument for the position of \S\ref{sec:protocol}: the
identical-pair check as presently applied does not merely fail to identify a
legal metric, it actively selects against the family that has the property being
sought. A metric that saturates on identical input is easy to build, since every
overlap function does it by construction, and saturation is evidence about
calibration at one endpoint, not about whether the metric reads the law. It
should be reported, and it should not be a filter.

\paragraph{The two orderings come apart.}
Over the \nDissoc\ neural baselines the rank correlation between calibrated $r$
on \textsc{FrJudge} and \textsc{LegalEdit} margin is $\rho=\dissocRho$, with a
$95\%$ bootstrap interval of $[\dissocRhoLo, \dissocRhoHi]$. At this number of
metrics, correlation with the human label simply does not predict legal
sensitivity, in either direction. The extremes show why it matters: the trivial
length feature is the best-correlating signal on the corpus ($r=0.641$,
Table~\ref{tab:baselines}) and has the lowest margin of anything we test
($0.011$, and it ranks the edited pair correctly only $11.3\%$ of the time),
while bidirectional NLI sits mid-table on correlation ($r=0.395$) and is the
only family that reacts to the edit at all. A metric selected on corpus
correlation alone would pick the wrong one. Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation} plots
the two axes against each other, and the emptiness of its upper-right quadrant
is the finding.

\section{From Auditing a Metric to Training One}
\label{sec:objectives}

The protocol so far audits scorers that already exist. It also has implications
for how one would fit a new one, and this section tests two of them. The first
follows from \S\ref{sec:corpus}: the gold label is the mean of five ratings
whose median spread is $\medianRange$ points, so fitting a scalar regressor to
that mean asks the model to predict a quantity none of the annotators asserted,
and rewards predicting the centre. The second follows from
\S\ref{sec:protocol}: if a diagnostic identifies a deficiency, the obvious
response is to train against it. We therefore train five variants on identical
splits, differing only in target and loss:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Scalar} (the conventional objective): MSE against the mean rating.
\item \textbf{Distributional}: a ten-way head trained with KL divergence against
      the empirical rating histogram, following \citet{uma2021learning} and
      \citet{peterson2019human}. The reported score is the distribution's
      expectation, so it is directly comparable, but the model additionally
      exposes its spread.
\item \textbf{Annotator-aware}: five regression heads, one per annotator,
      averaged at inference. This lets the model represent the lenient and
      strict populations separately instead of averaging them away.
\item \textbf{Quantile}: a pinball loss \citep{koenker1978regression} at
      $\tau=0.25$. The asymmetry is deliberate. A metric that
      \emph{overshoots} the human rating is permissive of bad simplifications,
      while one that undershoots is merely strict, and in a legal setting those
      errors are not symmetric; a quantile objective encodes that preference in
      the loss rather than hoping it emerges.
\item \textbf{Multi-task}: the scalar head plus an auxiliary characterization
      head, trained against the majority vote of the five annotators, which
      spans 17 of the rubric's 18 classes (the eighteenth never carries a
      majority). The characterization labels are collected in \textsc{FrJudge}
      and then discarded; since the published rubric has annotators characterize
      a clause \emph{before} scoring it, the label plausibly carries information
      about the score.
\end{itemize}

We additionally train two augmented variants: \textbf{DA}, the
identical/unrelated augmentation in current use carried over unchanged, and
\textbf{DA+LegalEdit}, which adds minimally-edited negatives to the training
mixture.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.} Correlation and RMSE are computed \emph{only}
on the human-annotated test split, never on a split containing augmentation.
This matters: adding identical pairs (label 10) and unrelated pairs (label 1) to
the evaluation set inflates label variance at the extremes, which raises Pearson
$r$ for every metric without any improvement in judgment. Published tables that
do not separate the two show the signature clearly: BERTScore moves from $0.46$
to $0.94$ while its RMSE simultaneously \emph{worsens} from $3.61$ to $5.09$,
which is the mark of variance inflation rather than of better measurement. Augmentation here affects
training only; the three diagnostic regimes are reported separately as pass
rates.

\paragraph{The training target barely moves the correlation.}
Table~\ref{tab:models} reports the grid, and the first result is a negative one.
The distributional head gains $0.030$ of Pearson $r$ over the scalar baseline
and the quantile head $0.011$, while the annotator-aware and multi-task heads
lose $0.008$ and $0.023$. Every one of those differences is smaller than the
scalar baseline's own spread across seeds ($\pm0.082$), and on paired seeds the
best of them wins three times out of five. \S\ref{sec:baselines} put the
smallest resolvable gap on a $\testN$-item test split at roughly a quarter of a
correlation point; that applies to our variants as much as to anyone else's
baselines, and we report the ordering without asking the reader to believe it.

The differences that do survive are in the other columns. The distributional
head is markedly steadier than the scalar head it replaces, $\pm0.029$ against
$\pm0.082$ over the same five seeds, reaches the best RMSE in the table
($1.99$), and roughly doubles the rank correlation ($\rho=0.578$ against
$0.280$), which is what one expects from a target carrying the shape of the
rating distribution and not only its mean. The quantile head does precisely what
it was asked to do: it overshoots the human label on $9.9\%$ of test items
against $25.4\%$ for the scalar head, the lowest rate of any model here. The
risk asymmetry that a legal setting calls for can therefore be written into the
loss rather than hoped for, and it costs nothing in correlation. We read the
disagreement-aware objectives as buying stability and calibration, not accuracy.

\paragraph{Augmentation, scored honestly, is harmful.}
The two augmented mixtures are the clearest effect in the table and they run
against the published finding. \textsc{JudgeBERT}-DA loses $0.350$ of
correlation against the un-augmented baseline and \textsc{DA+LegalEdit} loses
$0.420$, on five seeds out of five in both cases, and the instability is severe:
DA varies by $\pm0.254$ across seeds and reaches $r=-0.188$ on one of them. The
published figure has augmentation raising $r$ from $0.74$ to $0.97$. The
difference is entirely the evaluation set. That figure is computed on a split
containing the augmentation, where identical pairs at $10$ and unrelated pairs
at $1$ supply most of the label variance; scored on the human-annotated items
alone, the same augmentation costs more than half the correlation. This is the
concrete form of the variance-inflation argument above, and it is why we
separate the two evaluations rather than reporting a single number.

\paragraph{Trained constraints do not cross a change of document.}
Every model in Table~\ref{tab:models} scores $0\%$ on the identical-pair check,
the two augmented ones included, although identical pairs are in their training
mixture. The unrelated check goes the other way: the augmented models pass it on
$77\%$ and $69\%$ of probes and the un-augmented models on none. The asymmetry
is exactly the one our probe design predicts. Our unrelated probes are drawn
from the same two statutes the training mixture draws its unrelated pairs from,
so passing that check is an in-distribution result; our identical probes are
statutory sentences while the mixture's identical pairs are \textsc{FrJudge}
clauses, so passing that one would require generalising across source documents,
and no model does. The same holds for the diagnostic: \textsc{DA+LegalEdit},
trained on minimally-edited negatives, reaches a margin of $0.051$, against
$0.670$ for untrained bidirectional NLI. Its negatives are generated from
\textsc{FrJudge} originals and its probes from statutes, and the sensitivity
does not survive the move. One column here should be read with care:
\textsc{LegalEdit} false-acceptance is the share of flipped pairs still scored
$\geq 7$, so it is confounded with where a model puts its predictions overall.
The distributional head's $69\%$ reflects a head that scores high in general
(its identical probes average $7.22$), and the $0\%$ of the scalar heads
reflects models that score low on everything, not models that reject the edit.

\section{What Survives a Change of Document}
\label{sec:general}

A metric meta-evaluation that never changes source document cannot distinguish
legal sensitivity from familiarity with a form. We therefore add an
out-of-domain split. \textsc{FrJudge} clauses come from identifiable source
documents, which we recover from the raw export. Random splitting lets clauses
from the same insurance form appear in both training and test, and insurance
forms are highly templated. We compare the standard random split against a
document-grouped split in which no source document straddles the boundary
(Table~\ref{tab:models_grouped}); the difference estimates how much of the
reported performance is form-specific. We rerun three of the seven
configurations of Table~\ref{tab:models} on that split: the scalar baseline and
the two augmented mixtures. The remaining four differ from the baseline in what
they are trained to predict rather than in what they can exploit about a source
form, so the leakage they are exposed to is the same, and running them again
would cost training time without addressing the question the split is asked to
answer.

The corpus makes that harder than it sounds. Its 297 pairs come from only 21
source forms and the three largest hold 71\% of them, so the obvious
construction, sweeping the forms in random order and cutting at the cumulative
60/70\% marks, lets a single large form overshoot a boundary and starve the
split behind it. On seeds 42--46 that procedure returns test splits of 16, 87,
30, 0 and 88 pairs, one of them empty and one far too small to carry a
correlation. We therefore choose the form-to-split assignment by a short local
search over total deviation from the target sizes. Thousands of assignments hit
$178/30/89$ exactly, so each seed still selects a genuinely different partition
while every split matches the random-split sizes of Table~\ref{tab:models} pair
for pair, and the two tables differ only in which forms are held out. One
limitation is structural rather than incidental: the largest form is 37\% of the
corpus, larger than the 30\% test quota, so it always falls in training and the
grouped test set is always drawn from the remaining 188 pairs.

\paragraph{The comparison does not resolve.} The scalar baseline's correlation
falls from $0.542$ on the random split to $0.454$ on the grouped one, a drop of
$0.088$ that is in the direction leakage would predict. It does not survive
inspection. The per-seed differences are $+0.169$, $-0.097$, $-0.416$, $-0.182$
and $+0.086$: two of the five seeds \emph{improve} under the harder split, and
the grouped spread ($\pm0.143$) is wider than the gap itself. The two augmented
mixtures move by $-0.002$ and $+0.026$, which is to say not at all. As a
control, the untrained NLI reference is fitted to nothing and should be
indifferent to how the corpus is partitioned; it moves from $0.395$ to $0.391$,
confirming that the two splits are otherwise comparable and that the variance
above is the models' and not the split's.

We therefore do not claim that document-level leakage inflates the reported
correlation, and we would ask that our own evidence be held to the standard
\S\ref{sec:baselines} applies elsewhere. What we can say is narrower and, we
think, more useful to anyone planning a successor corpus: \textsc{FrJudge} is
too small and too concentrated to answer the question. Its grouped test split is
always drawn from the $188$ pairs the largest form leaves behind, at which size
the seed-to-seed variation swamps the effect being measured. A corpus intended
to support an out-of-domain claim needs its documents spread far more evenly
than three forms holding $71\%$ of the data, and this is the aspect of the
design we would change first.

\paragraph{Stratifying by length change.}
We further break the test predictions down by the magnitude of the length change
between original and simplification. If a metric's correlation is concentrated
in the buckets where the simplification is much shorter, it is detecting
deletion rather than legal change, the hypothesis raised in
\S\ref{sec:baselines}. Table~\ref{tab:buckets} reports Pearson $r$ inside each
tercile of $|\,|s|-|o|\,|$. Within a tercile the length signal is largely held
constant, so a metric that retains its correlation there is using something
other than deletion; one whose correlation collapses was reading length all
along. This is a weaker form of the same dissociation logic as
\S\ref{sec:protocol}: it holds a confound approximately fixed by conditioning
rather than exactly fixed by construction.

\caption{Correlation with the human label \emph{within} terciles of the

The result qualifies \S\ref{sec:baselines} in a way that is worth stating
carefully, because it cuts against our own argument. The length feature's
correlation is overwhelmingly a \emph{between}-tercile effect: pooled over the
corpus it reaches $0.613$, but inside the small and medium terciles it falls to
$0.243$ and $0.165$. Once items of comparable length change are compared with
each other, knowing the length change tells you little. It recovers to $0.585$
only in the tercile where the simplification is much shorter than the original,
which is exactly where deletion is available to be detected. The neural
baselines have the opposite profile: in the medium tercile \textsc{BERTScore}
over FlauBERT reaches $0.449$ and LaBSE $0.532$, at or above their pooled values
of $0.484$ and $0.424$, where the length feature retains almost nothing. The
supervised surface ridge tracks the raw length feature bucket for bucket, which
is what one would expect if it is largely a length model with extra terms. The
small tercile is hard for everything, which we read as a floor effect rather
than as a result: when the two sentences are close to the same length, none of
these signals has much to go on.

The honest reading is therefore narrower than ``legal meaning preservation is
length preservation''. The corpus-level ranking is dominated by length, and any
comparison that omits a length control will attribute to a metric what it should
attribute to that confound. But the neural metrics are not \emph{only} reading
length. Where the length signal is weakest, they are the ones that hold up,
keeping or exceeding the correlation they had over the whole corpus; whatever
they are reading in that stratum, it is not the length change. What
\S\ref{sec:results} adds is that the residual signal they retain is still not
legal sensitivity, since the same metrics move almost not at all when the law is
changed and the length is held exactly fixed. The two analyses answer different
questions, and a metric has to survive both.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Three checks, none of them a filter.}
We propose that a metric claiming to measure legal meaning report three checks,
not two: identical pairs (does it saturate correctly?), unrelated pairs (does it
bottom out?), and \textsc{LegalEdit} (does it move when, and only when, the law
moves?). The first two are jointly satisfiable by lexical overlap and so cannot
distinguish a legal metric from a similarity function; the third cannot be
passed by overlap at all. That \textsc{LegalEdit} is passable matters as much as
that it is hard: because bidirectional NLI clears it while every similarity
metric does not, a low margin identifies a specific, fixable deficiency rather
than an inherent limit of automatic evaluation.

Our second recommendation is one the results force on us. The three checks
should be reported \emph{jointly and separately}, and none of them should be
used as an admission criterion. Here the metric that scores highest on the
diagnostic is also the metric that fails the identical-pair check most badly,
and a pipeline that screened candidates on saturation, as the current convention
invites, would have discarded it before the interesting question was asked.
Saturation, bottoming out, and legal sensitivity are three independent
properties; a scorer can be repaired for the first two by calibration, and
cannot be repaired for the third at all. Rank on the property that cannot be
fixed.

We also caution that a metric trained on its own sanity checks has not thereby
passed them, and our runs show that caution cutting harder than we expected.
\textsc{JudgeBERT}-DA is reported at $100\%$ on both checks, with those exact
distributions in its training mixture. Our augmented models pass the unrelated
check on $77\%$ and $69\%$ of probes and the identical check on none of them
(Table~\ref{tab:models}), and the only difference between those two outcomes is
whether the probe comes from the same documents as the training pairs. Training
on a constraint buys satisfaction of it in distribution and nothing at all
across a change of source. The honest report separates ``satisfies the
constraint by construction'' from ``generalises to the constraint'', and only
the second is evidence about a metric.

\paragraph{What to report alongside correlation.}
Given a corpus with $k$ ratings per item, the human ceiling is free to compute
and should be mandatory. We recommend reporting (i) leave-one-annotator-out
correlation as the ``as good as an expert'' bound, (ii) the Spearman--Brown
reliability of the aggregate as the ``as good as the aggregate allows'' bound,
(iii) a trivial surface-feature baseline, and (iv) all metrics calibrated to the
human scale under identical supervision. Each of these changed a conclusion in
this study, which is our main reason for proposing them as a protocol rather
than as advice.

\paragraph{Entailment is the better starting point.}
The construct \textsc{FrJudge} annotates is defined by an error taxonomy whose
two dominant categories are omission and hallucination. Those are precisely the
two directions of entailment failure, and our results suggest the correspondence
is not merely conceptual: the only scorer that reacts to a legally decisive edit
is the one built from bidirectional entailment. We would not conclude that an
off-the-shelf multilingual NLI model is an adequate legal metric: its
correlation with the human label is middling ($r=0.395$), it is slower than a
fine-tuned encoder, and its margin of $\maxMargin$ still leaves a third of the
range unspent. The conclusion we draw is narrower and, we think, more useful:
the next legal-meaning metric should be built on an entailment objective and
evaluated on the diagnostic, rather than on a similarity objective selected by
corpus correlation. A supervised model trained against \textsc{FrJudge}
\emph{and} minimally-edited negatives is the obvious candidate, and
\S\ref{sec:objectives} reports a first attempt at one.

\paragraph{Disagreement is the finding, not the noise.}
That two of five Quebec law students systematically rate legal preservation
three points lower than the other three is not an annotation defect to be
averaged away. It is a measurement of how much legal interpretation of an
isolated clause varies among trained readers, and any deployed metric that
returns a single number conceals it. A metric that reports ``6.2, experts ranged
2--9'' tells a practitioner something actionable, namely that this clause needs
a human, which ``6.2'' does not.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

Every number in this paper is produced by a single pipeline that runs end to end
on a laptop; the largest model involved is CamemBERTv2-base (112M parameters)
and no GPU cluster is required. We release the experiment code, the
reconstruction of \textsc{FrJudge} from the raw export (which recovers annotator
identity and source document, both dropped from the public JSONL), the
document-grouped splits, the \textsc{LegalEdit} generator and scoring harness,
and the result files behind every table.

One caveat on exact reproduction: our training runs use Apple MPS kernels, which
are not bitwise deterministic, so a fixed seed reproduces a run only
approximately: repeat runs of the scalar configuration at seed $42$ differ in
test-set $r$ by several hundredths, which is comparable to the spread across
seeds. We therefore report means and standard deviations over five seeds and
treat any difference smaller than that spread as unresolved, the same discipline
\S\ref{sec:baselines} asks of everyone else, applied to ourselves. Anyone
reproducing this on CUDA should expect tighter agreement and correspondingly
narrower error bars.

Two further details matter for anyone reproducing the diagnostic. The first is
what the probes share with training, which differs by regime and which we state
precisely because conflating the two is one of the failure modes the protocol is
meant to prevent. The \emph{identical} probe is built from statutory sentences
(the Quebec Automobile Insurance Act and the Highway Safety Code) while the
identical pairs in the \textsc{-DA} training mixture are built from
\textsc{FrJudge}'s insurance-form clauses, so a model passes that probe only by
generalising across source documents. The \textsc{LegalEdit} probe is likewise
generated from statutory sentences, while the minimally-edited negatives added
by the \textsc{DA+LegalEdit} variant are generated from \textsc{FrJudge}
originals: same perturbation rules, disjoint sentences. The \emph{unrelated}
probe is the weakest of the three in this respect: both it and the unrelated
pairs in the training mixture are drawn from the same two statutes, under
different random seeds, so a high unrelated pass rate for a \textsc{-DA} model
should be read as in-distribution and we do not treat it as evidence of
generalisation. Second, the ordinal Krippendorff coefficient we report is
computed with the same library and the same reliability matrix as the nominal
one, so the difference between $0.104$ and $\alphaOrd$ is attributable to the
distance function alone.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

Our \textsc{LegalEdit} labels are definitional rather than annotated: we assert
that changing \emph{doit} to \emph{peut} changes legal meaning, rather than
having lawyers rate each perturbation. We think this is defensible for tier-1
edits, which alter modality, party, polarity or quantum, and we report tier-2
scope shifts separately for exactly this reason; but a human-validated version
would be stronger, and a small expert study is the obvious next step. The
perturbations are also single-edit and template-generated, so they probe a
specific and narrow kind of legal change; a system could in principle be tuned
to them without acquiring general legal sensitivity, which is why we treat
\textsc{LegalEdit} as a necessary condition and not a benchmark to be optimised.

That caveat bears directly on our most encouraging result, and we would rather
state it than let a reader find it. Our perturbations are largely negation,
modality, quantifier and antonym contrasts, which is close to the inventory
natural language inference training data is built around. A model fine-tuned on
XNLI has seen a great many \emph{ne \dots pas} insertions and
\emph{tous}/\emph{certains} alternations, so bidirectional NLI's margin of
$\maxMargin$ may reflect familiarity with the \emph{form} of our edits rather
than sensitivity to their legal consequence. The two hypotheses make different
predictions: only the second should survive perturbations that change legal
force without changing logical form, such as substituting a defined term for a
near-synonym that is not its legal equivalent. Separating them needs a version
of the diagnostic built around defined-term substitution, which our rule
inventory reaches only through the single \emph{automobile}/\emph{v\'ehicule}
case. We report the NLI result as a promising direction with an identified
confound, not as evidence that entailment models read law.

A further mismatch is that the diagnostic and the correlation evaluation run on
different text. \textsc{FrJudge} is drawn from insurance forms, while the
\textsc{LegalEdit} probes are generated from two Quebec statutes, because the
statutes supply the volume and the regularity of phrasing that the perturbation
rules need. The two are the same language and register but not the same genre,
so Table~\ref{tab:challenge} and Table~\ref{tab:baselines} answer different
questions about the same metric and should not be read as one ranking.

We do not evaluate an LLM-as-judge baseline, and this is the most significant
gap in our comparison. Recent legal evaluation work \citep{zhou2024lemaj}
suggests that a prompted frontier model given the annotators' own rubric is the
natural competitor to a fine-tuned 112M-parameter encoder, and a demonstration
that the small supervised model matches it would be a strong efficiency result.
We note two reasons the omission does not affect our conclusions. Our claims are
about what a corpus-correlation comparison establishes, and about a diagnostic
property, sensitivity to a legally decisive edit, that is a necessary condition
for any scorer, prompted or fine-tuned; and our evaluation harness
takes an arbitrary scoring function, so \textsc{LegalEdit} can be run against a
prompted judge without modification. We release the harness with that interface
so the comparison can be completed.

Like the corpus we build on, we evaluate isolated clauses out of document
context, and all simplifications come from a single generator
(\texttt{gpt-4-turbo}) with one prompt. A metric meta-evaluation properly
requires outputs from several systems spanning a range of quality; with one
generator, a metric may be learning that generator's characteristic failure
modes. \S\ref{sec:general} addresses document-level leakage but cannot address
generator-level overfitting.

Finally, our human ceiling is computed on all 297 items while the model
correlations are computed on $\testN$-item test splits, and the ceiling carries
its own sampling uncertainty (per-annotator 95\% CIs in
Table~\ref{tab:ceiling}). The comparison ``model $>$ ceiling'' should be read
with those intervals in view.

\section{Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:ethics}

\paragraph{Annotator de-anonymisation in the public artefacts.}
\textsc{FrJudge}'s five annotators are pseudonymised as A--E in the paper that
introduces them. The public raw export, however, records their real first names
in the \texttt{\_annotator\_id} and \texttt{\_session\_id} fields, joined to
per-annotation Unix timestamps and free-text justifications. This makes five
named law students' individual legal judgments, working pace, and reasoning
publicly attributable, contrary to the pseudonymisation the paper describes. We
use only pseudonyms A--E throughout this work, our released code pseudonymises
on load, and we do not reproduce the names. We have not been able to determine
whether the annotators' consent covered named release; we flag it so that the
maintainers can repair the file and, if appropriate, its history. We report this
as a reproducibility and data-protection issue, not as a criticism of the
study's conduct: releasing raw annotation exports is good practice, and this is
a foreseeable and easily corrected side effect of it.

\paragraph{Use of the metric.}
Our results strengthen a caution \citet{beauchemin2025judgebert} themselves raise. A metric
that cannot distinguish \emph{doit} from \emph{peut} must not be used as an
automated gate on whether a simplified clause is safe to publish. We regard the
appropriate deployment as triage, that is, the ranking of clauses for human
review and the flagging of disagreement, rather than as certification. Nothing here constitutes legal
advice.

\paragraph{Notes on the public artefacts.}
Working from the released files surfaced a number of small discrepancies, which
we record here so that they can be corrected, and offer as errata rather than as
criticism. Table~7 reports ``Top K 0.9'', but $0.9$ is a \texttt{top\_p} value
and the OpenAI API exposes no \texttt{top\_k}; \S3.2.2 describes Kandel and
Moles (1958) as a ``Flesch-Kincaid grade level'' with $\leq 50$ indicating
harder text and a discarded example at $69.87$, which identifies it as French
Flesch \emph{Reading Ease} (higher = easier), not a grade level; \S6.2 says
``only three metrics'' and lists four; Table~5's fourth column is headed
\texttt{\%>1\%} while the text defines it as the ratio scoring at or below
$1\%$; and SBERT-Multi's identical-pair score differs between the DA-false
($0.00$) and DA-true ($100.00$) blocks although it is an untrained metric whose
behaviour on identical pairs cannot depend on how \textsc{JudgeBERT} was
trained. The repository README additionally documents a schema from a different
project (a binary grammaticality label), and the dataset license differs between
the paper (CC-BY 4.0) and the repository (CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0).

\section{Conclusion}

Legal meaning preservation is the right construct to evaluate, and the resources
that now exist for it deserve to be built on. But the evidence a metric paper in
this area conventionally offers, namely a correlation with an averaged human
label, computed against uncalibrated baselines, without a trivial control, and
on sanity checks the model may have been trained on, cannot establish that the
metric measures legal meaning rather than lexical overlap and length. We have argued that the
missing ingredient is a dissociation, and that it is cheap: hold surface form
fixed and move the law. On that test every similarity-based metric we examined,
the state of the art included, scores a sentence that says the opposite of the
original as though nothing had changed, and reversing which party a clause binds
is among the least detectable edits of all. The test is not unpassable:
bidirectional entailment, a family the standard comparison leaves out and the
standard sanity check would have disqualified, clears most of it. That is the
encouraging half of the result, and it points at what the next version of such a
metric should be made of. It also carries a warning about how these metrics are
selected, because the signal that ranks best on the corpus ranks last on the
law.

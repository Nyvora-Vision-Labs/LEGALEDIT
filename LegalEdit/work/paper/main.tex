\begin{abstract}
An automatic metric for legal meaning preservation should answer a practical
question: does a simplified clause say what the original said? The automated
checks in current use cannot establish that it does. Requiring an identical pair
to score highest and an unrelated pair lowest moves lexical overlap and legal
force together, so any monotone function of token overlap satisfies both by
construction. We propose a remedy in the form of a \emph{dissociation}: an
evaluation item on which surface form is held fixed while legal force moves. We
release it as \textsc{LegalEdit}, \numedits\ minimal perturbations of Quebec
statutory French (modal, party, polarity, magnitude, scope) that reverse legal
force while preserving \meanjaccard\ of the tokens. \textsc{LegalEdit} splits
the metric field in two. Every similarity-based scorer we test spends only
$\simMarginLo$--$\bertMargin$ of its identical-to-unrelated range on an edit
that reverses the law; bidirectional NLI entailment spends $\maxMargin$ and
ranks $93\%$ of items correctly. Awkwardly for current practice, the family that
clears the diagnostic is the one family the identical-pair check would have
disqualified. We situate the diagnostic in a four-part validation
protocol --- a measured human ceiling, calibration under identical supervision,
a trivial surface-feature control, and the dissociation --- and instantiate all
four on \textsc{FrJudge}, the only annotated corpus for this construct. Corpus
correlation and legal sensitivity come apart there: the best-correlating signal
is a ten-feature surface regression that is worst on the diagnostic, so
correlation alone selects the wrong metric. Code, splits and the diagnostic are
released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Rewriting a legal text for a lay reader has an unusual failure mode. When a news
summary drops a qualifier the reader is mildly misinformed; when an insurance
clause drops a qualifier the reader may be uninsured. The 2024 decision in
\emph{Moffatt v.\ Air Canada}, which held an airline liable for its chatbot's
misstatement of its own bereavement-fare policy, established that the gap
between what a document says and what an automated system says it says is
legally cognisable and not merely academic.

If simplified legal text is produced automatically, something has to check it,
and that something will in practice be an automatic metric. What a practitioner
needs to know of it is narrower and harder than correlation with human ratings
on naturally occurring simplifications: does the score move when the legal
effect of the clause moves, and does it stay put when the wording changes
without legal effect? Neither is measured by a correlation coefficient. Worse,
the validation apparatus the field has converged on cannot, even in
principle, distinguish a metric that reads law from a metric that counts shared
words. Two automated checks are in current use \citep{beauchemin2023meaningbert,
beauchemin2025judgebert}: a sentence paired with itself must receive the maximum
score, and a sentence paired with an unrelated one the minimum. Both are
necessary and both are easy to state. But moving from an identical pair to an
unrelated pair changes lexical overlap and legal meaning \emph{together}, in the
same direction, at the same time. Any monotone function of token overlap
therefore satisfies both checks by construction, which is exactly why the
embedding metrics in the literature already pass them. The checks test
calibration at two endpoints; they do not test what the metric is reading.

The remedy is a \emph{dissociation}: an evaluation item on which the two
confounded variables are pulled apart, so that legal force changes while surface
form does not. Consider a clause in which \emph{doit} (must) is replaced by
\emph{peut} (may). Ninety-odd percent of the tokens are untouched, the sentence
remains fluent and statutory in register, and an obligation has become a
permission. A metric that measures legal meaning must drop sharply; a metric
that measures overlap cannot. We build \textsc{LegalEdit}, \numedits\ such
perturbations over Quebec statutory French, and release it with a harness taking
an arbitrary scoring function, so that untrained metrics, fine-tuned regressors
and prompted judges can all be administered the same test.

\textsc{LegalEdit} separates the metric landscape cleanly
(\S\ref{sec:results}). The similarity metrics collapse, spending
$\simMarginLo$--$\bertMargin$ of their working range on the edit; bidirectional
NLI entailment spends $\maxMargin$. The diagnostic is therefore hard but not
impossible, which makes a low score a fixable deficiency rather than an inherent
limit of automatic evaluation --- and, awkwardly for current practice, the
family that clears it is the one the identical-pair check would disqualify,
since multilingual NLI scores a sentence paired with itself at $\nliIdentRaw$
rather than $1$.

A diagnostic alone does not discipline how corpus-correlation evidence is read,
so we assemble three further requirements around it (\S\ref{sec:protocol}): a
\emph{measured} human ceiling, free to compute whenever a corpus ships several
ratings per item; comparison under \emph{identical supervision}, since an
unsupervised similarity score and a regressor fitted to the label are not
otherwise on the same scale; and a \emph{trivial-feature control}, because the
surface statistics of a simplification, above all how much shorter it is, carry
more of the human label than is usually assumed. We instantiate all four on
\textsc{FrJudge} \citep{beauchemin2025judgebert}, 297 Quebec insurance clauses
with five expert ratings each, and the only annotated corpus for this construct
(\S\ref{sec:casestudy}). It supports a measurable human ceiling of $r=\ceilR$;
a ridge regression over ten surface features reaches $r=\surfR$ on the same
split, at that ceiling and above every neural baseline; and the rank correlation
between corpus performance and \textsc{LegalEdit} margin is only
$\rho=\dissocRho$ ($95\%$ CI $[\dissocRhoLo, \dissocRhoHi]$), so corpus
correlation does not predict legal sensitivity in either direction.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i) \textsc{LegalEdit}, a \numedits-item dissociation diagnostic defined by
legal effect rather than by linguistic category, with a scoring harness and the
margin statistic it is read with; (ii) an argument that the sanity checks in
current use are jointly satisfiable by lexical overlap, and a four-part protocol
that repairs this; (iii) an empirical separation of the field into a similarity
family that fails the diagnostic and an entailment family that largely clears
it; and (iv) the first human-ceiling estimate for legal meaning preservation,
with a calibrated baseline comparison including trivial surface features.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Evaluating text simplification.}
Simplification quality is conventionally decomposed into fluency, simplicity and
meaning preservation, and metrics borrowed from translation and summarisation
transfer poorly to the third. \citet{sulem2018bleu} show BLEU
\citep{papineni2002bleu} does not correlate with meaning preservation once
sentence splitting is involved, the operation most characteristic of legal
simplification \citep{garimella2022text}; SARI
\citep{xu-etal-2016-optimizing} targets simplicity rather than adequacy, against
general-domain references \citep{alvamanchego2020asset}. Embedding metrics
\citep{zhang2019bertscore,reimers2019sbert} capture semantic variability better
but, as \citet{cripwell2024document} and \citet{kew2023bless} document, score
consistently high across \emph{all} alterations, penalising even substantial
meaning changes too little. Trained metrics such as LENS
\citep{maddela2023lens} and MeaningBERT \citep{beauchemin2023meaningbert}
improve correlation on the corpora they are fitted to.
\citet{agrawal2024meaning} argue for grounding meaning preservation in reading
comprehension rather than similarity, and \citet{trienes2024infolossqa}
characterise simplification errors as recoverable \emph{information loss},
close to the omission category that dominates legal error taxonomies.

\paragraph{French and legal resources.}
French simplification corpora are few and none are legal: Alector, CLEAR
\citep{grabar2018clear} and WikiLarge-FR \citep{cardon2020wikilargefr} cover
literacy, medical and encyclopaedic text. Legal NLP resources are mostly English
and mostly not about simplification \citep{hendrycks2021cuad,garneau2022criminal}.
\citet{hagan2023good} proposes criteria a legal AI system should be benchmarked
against, two of which --- that a response covers details and exceptions, and
that it does not misrepresent the substantive law --- form the basis of the
construct \citet{beauchemin2025judgebert} operationalise in \textsc{FrJudge},
the corpus we take as our case study and, to our knowledge, still the only
annotated resource for legal meaning preservation in any language.

\paragraph{Challenge sets and entailment.}
Our diagnostic follows work showing that correlation on naturally occurring
outputs hides brittleness on targeted perturbations:
\citet{sai2021perturbation} build perturbation checklists for NLG metrics,
\citet{anschutz2023negation} show metrics systematically miss negation, and
\citet{chen2023menli} demonstrate that similarity-based metrics are not robust
to meaning-changing edits while NLI-based metrics repair much of the gap.
\textsc{LegalEdit} adapts the method to a domain where the perturbations are not
linguistic curiosities but the exact operations that decide coverage disputes,
and where the inventory is therefore organised by legal effect rather than by
linguistic category; to our knowledge no prior challenge set targets legal
force. The entailment side of that literature is well developed for English
adequacy \citep{laban2022summac,zha2023alignscore,fabbri2022qafacteval,rebuffel2021questeval},
which is why it is absent from the French literature. We construct a French
analogue by running a multilingual NLI model
\citep{laurer2024nli,conneau2020xlmr} in both directions, so that failure of
$o \Rightarrow s$ signals hallucination and failure of $s \Rightarrow o$ signals
omission --- the two dominant categories of the legal error taxonomy, which is
why we treat this family as the strongest available starting point rather than
as one baseline among many.

\paragraph{Disagreement and meta-evaluation.}
The assumption that a single gold label exists has been extensively challenged
\citep{pavlick2019inherent,plank2022problem,uma2021learning,peterson2019human}.
Legal interpretation is a paradigm case, since disagreement between competent
lawyers is the ordinary condition of the field rather than annotator error, and
we accordingly treat the five ratings per item as a distribution to be modelled
(\S\ref{sec:objectives}). On the statistical side,
\citet{deutsch2021statistical} show correlation estimates for generation metrics
carry wide confidence intervals and that apparent metric differences frequently
fail significance testing, \citet{graham2014williams} give the appropriate test
for dependent correlations, and \citet{card2020power} show NLP comparisons are
routinely underpowered. Our protocol applies all three as a matter of course.

\section{What Would Validate a Legal Meaning Metric?}
\label{sec:protocol}

We first set out what we think the evidence should consist of. The four
requirements below are stated for an arbitrary scorer $m(o,s) \in \mathbb{R}$
taking an original clause $o$ and a candidate simplification $s$; none
presuppose an architecture, and all four are cheap enough that we see no reason
for a metric paper to omit them.

\subsection{Requirement 1: a measured ceiling}

A correlation with human judgment means nothing until one knows what correlation
a human achieves. Where a corpus carries $k \geq 3$ ratings per item this is
free: hold out one rater, correlate their ratings against the mean of the
remaining $k-1$, and average over the held-out position. Two bounds should be
reported, because they answer different questions. The leave-one-annotator-out
correlation answers \emph{is the metric as good as an expert?} The
Spearman--Brown reliability of the $k$-rater mean, and its square root, answer
\emph{is the metric as good as the aggregate label allows?} A metric can sit above the first and below
the second without being superhuman at anything, because predicting an average
is easier than being one of the raters averaged: aggregation cancels
idiosyncratic variance no individual can cancel alone. Reporting one bound and
not the other invites the conflation we document in \S\ref{sec:ceiling}.

\subsection{Requirement 2: identical supervision}

Comparisons in this literature routinely place an unsupervised cosine similarity
and a regressor fine-tuned on the label in the same table, normalise the former
by decimal scaling into the label range, and report RMSE for both. Decimal
scaling is not calibration: it fixes the range but neither the location nor the
shape of the distribution. A cosine similarity occupying $[0.7,1.0]$ maps to
high scores everywhere and records a large RMSE however well it ranks items, so
the resulting column measures access to supervision rather than judgment. The
repair is to fit a monotone map from each metric's raw score to the human scale
on the training split only, and to evaluate on the held-out split. We use
isotonic regression \citep{zadrozny2002transforming}, which cannot improve a
metric's ranking because it is monotone; that rank statistics and Pearson $r$
are unaffected for the unsupervised metrics is a useful check that the map does
only what it is supposed to.

\subsection{Requirement 3: a trivial-feature control}

Simplification changes surface statistics systematically, and the most
systematic of them is length: LLM simplifications are usually shorter, and the
dominant annotated error category in legal simplification is omission. A rubric
deducting score once per identified error therefore produces a label partly
predictable from how many words disappeared, with no representation of legal
force involved, and any comparison omitting a length control will attribute to a
neural metric what belongs to that confound. We require a supervised baseline
over surface features alone (word counts, their difference, token and numeral
overlap), fitted under Requirement 2 and reported in the same table.

\subsection{Requirement 4: dissociation}

The three requirements above discipline the corpus-correlation evidence. They
cannot establish what a metric is reading, because on naturally occurring pairs
surface form and legal force vary together --- the same confound that makes the
identical/unrelated checks uninformative, and it needs the same remedy: an
evaluation set on which the two are pulled apart by construction.

Formally, let $s'$ be a perturbation of $s$ with token Jaccard
$J(s,s') \approx 1$ and legal force $L(s') \neq L(s)$, and let $u$ be an
unrelated sentence of the same register. We report the \emph{margin fraction}
\[
\text{margin} \;=\; \frac{\overline{m(s,s)} - \overline{m(s,s')}}
                          {\overline{m(s,s)} - \overline{m(s,u)}},
\]
the share of the metric's own identical-to-unrelated dynamic range that it
spends on a legally decisive edit. The normalisation makes the statistic
comparable across metrics with incomparable raw scales, and charges each metric
against the range it actually has rather than a nominal $[0,1]$. A metric that
treats the flipped sentence as it treats an unrelated one scores $1$; a pure
overlap function scores near $0$.

The margin is not the rate at which a metric ranks $(s,s)$ above $(s,s')$, and
the rate is much the weaker quantity: a metric can rank correctly on every item
while moving by a thousandth of its range, a ranking that carries no decision.
Nor is the margin redundant with corpus correlation; whether the two agree is an
empirical question, and \S\ref{sec:dissociation} reports that here they do not.

\section{\textsc{LegalEdit}: Constructing the Dissociation}
\label{sec:legaledit}

\subsection{Source text and edit inventory}

The perturbations need source sentences that are statutory in register,
plentiful, and regular enough in phrasing that a rule can apply cleanly. We draw
them from two Quebec statutes held out from any corpus used elsewhere in the
paper, the Automobile Insurance Act and the Highway Safety Code, and apply a
single minimal edit that changes legal force while leaving the sentence
otherwise intact. The inventory is organised by legal effect rather than by
linguistic category, which is the respect in which it departs from general-domain
perturbation suites:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Modality}: \emph{doit} $\leftrightarrow$ \emph{peut}; obligation
      becomes permission.
\item \textbf{Party}: \emph{l'assur\'e} $\leftrightarrow$ \emph{l'assureur};
      the duty changes hands.
\item \textbf{Polarity}: dropping \emph{ne \dots pas}; \emph{couvre}
      $\leftrightarrow$ \emph{ne couvre pas}; \emph{exclut} $\leftrightarrow$
      \emph{inclut}.
\item \textbf{Quantum}: the first monetary or temporal amount multiplied by ten
      (\emph{12 mois} $\rightarrow$ \emph{120 mois}).
\item \textbf{Scope}: \emph{tous les} $\rightarrow$ \emph{certains};
      \emph{sauf si} $\rightarrow$ \emph{si}.
\item \textbf{Temporal}: \emph{avant} $\leftrightarrow$ \emph{apr\`es}.
\item \textbf{Class} (tier 2): \emph{automobile} $\leftrightarrow$
      \emph{v\'ehicule}, the defining example of the construct; and \emph{et}
      $\leftrightarrow$ \emph{ou}, which converts conjunctive conditions to
      disjunctive ones.
\end{itemize}

We separate a \emph{tier 1} set, in which the edit unambiguously changes who is
bound, what is covered, or how much, from a \emph{tier 2} set of scope and class
shifts, and report both.

\subsection{Keeping the edit invisible to a fluency detector}

A diagnostic of this kind fails if a metric can find the edited item by noticing
that it reads badly. Perturbations introducing a grammatical artefact are
therefore filtered by a conservative French well-formedness check covering
failed elision (\emph{le automobile}), missing contraction (\emph{de le}),
doubled negation and determiner--noun gender disagreement. It flags none of the
$400$ unmodified source sentences, so it removes only genuine artefacts rather
than trimming the set toward any particular rule.

The result is \numedits\ pairs (\numeditsTierOne\ tier 1) with mean token
Jaccard \meanjaccard\ against their source, none below \minjaccard.
Table~\ref{tab:examples} in Appendix~\ref{app:extra} gives one example per
rule.


\subsection{Administering the diagnostic}

For each source sentence $s$ with perturbation $s'$ we score three pairs:
$(s,s)$, $(s,s')$, and $(s,u)$ for an unrelated sentence $u$ drawn from the same
statutes. The metric's scores are min--max normalised per metric so that the
identical and unrelated probes anchor its range, and the margin fraction of
\S\ref{sec:protocol} is computed from the three means. Because the harness takes
an arbitrary scoring function $m$, the same procedure applies unchanged to
untrained metrics, to fine-tuned regressors, and to a prompted LLM judge.

\subsection{The comparison set}
\label{sec:metriczoo}

We administer the diagnostic to French-capable metrics throughout, since scoring
English-only metrics on French text confounds language coverage with metric
quality: BERTScore \citep{zhang2019bertscore} over CamemBERTv2
\citep{antoun2024camembert2} and FlauBERT \citep{le2020flaubert}, both F1 and
recall-only, since recall directly targets omission; sentence-embedding cosine
with LaBSE \citep{feng2022labse}, Sentence-CamemBERT, multilingual mpnet, and
multilingual E5; and bidirectional NLI entailment with mDeBERTa-XNLI
\citep{laurer2024nli}. To these we add token Jaccard and the trivial surface
features of Requirement 3, so that the overlap end of the spectrum is
represented explicitly rather than by inference.

\section{Diagnostic Results: Two Families, Not One}
\label{sec:results}

Table~\ref{tab:challenge} reports the margin over \nMetricsChallenged\ scorers
(Figure~\ref{fig:challenge} in Appendix~\ref{app:extra} plots it), and the result is sharper than we expected. The
metrics divide cleanly into two families.

Every \emph{similarity}-based metric fails, and fails almost completely. The
seven embedding and BERTScore variants spend between $\simMarginLo$ and
$\bertMargin$ of their working range on an edit that reverses the law: BERTScore
over CamemBERTv2 places a sentence saying the opposite of the original at
$0.982$ on a scale where the original itself sits at $1.000$ and an unrelated
statute sits at $0.193$. Token Jaccard, at $0.079$, is in the same regime, which
is the point: these metrics are behaving as overlap functions, exactly as the
argument of \S\ref{sec:protocol} says they must. Their \emph{discrimination}
rates are uninformative for the same reason: they rank the identical pair above
the edited one $100\%$ of the time, but by a margin so small that it carries no
decision. This is the concrete case the margin statistic was defined to catch.

Bidirectional NLI does not fail. It spends $\maxMargin$ of its range on the
edit, ranks correctly on $93.3\%$ of items, and places a legally flipped
sentence ($0.265$) nearer the unrelated pair ($0.010$) than the identical one
($0.784$). The two directions individually reach $0.424$ and $0.473$; taking
their minimum, which is what an omission-\emph{or}-hallucination reading of the
construct requires, is what produces the $0.670$. On the property the construct
is about, this family is not merely competitive but in a different class.

\paragraph{Relation to \textsc{MENLI}.}
That entailment survives meaning-changing edits better than similarity is not a
new claim --- \citet{chen2023menli} establish it for general-domain NLG metrics
--- and our result confirms it. What is new is that the inventory is defined by
legal effect rather than by linguistic category, so the diagnostic speaks to a
deployment question (may this metric gate a simplified clause?) rather than to
metric robustness in general; that the dissociation in \S\ref{sec:dissociation}
is a property of this corpus and could not be read off \textsc{MENLI}; and that
the check this literature has standardised on would have excluded the entailment
family outright, as the next paragraph shows.

\paragraph{The existing checks would have excluded it.}
Note where NLI's identical score sits: $0.784$, not $1.000$. This is not a
normalisation artefact. In raw terms mDeBERTa assigns a mean entailment of
$\nliIdentRaw$ to a sentence paired with \emph{itself}, and clears the $99\%$
bar the identical-pair convention requires on only $\nliIdentPass\%$ of items,
against $100\%$ for every BERTScore and embedding metric. The metric that best
tracks legal meaning is therefore the one metric that convention would
\emph{disqualify}. We take this as the strongest available argument for the
position of \S\ref{sec:protocol}: the identical-pair check as presently applied
does not merely fail to identify a legal metric, it actively selects against the
family that has the property being sought. A metric that saturates on identical
input is easy to build, since every overlap function does it by construction,
and saturation is evidence about calibration at one endpoint, not about whether
the metric reads the law. It should be reported, and it should not be a filter.

\paragraph{Is the failure uniform across edit types?}
No rule is reliably detected. The best-detected operation, dropping \emph{ne
\dots pas}, still averages only $\bestRuleMargin$, and it is plausibly easiest
because it is the one rule that \emph{removes} tokens rather than substituting
them; at the other end, swapping the bound party (\emph{l'assur\'e} for
\emph{l'assureur}), which reverses who owes the duty and who is owed it, draws
$\partyMargin$ over the $\nParty$ party-swap items against a mean of
$\ruleMarginMean$ across all rules. Reversing which party a clause binds is
close to the least detectable thing one can do to it. Appendix~\ref{app:extra}
gives the full breakdown.

\section{Case Study: The Protocol on \textsc{FrJudge}}
\label{sec:casestudy}

The diagnostic answers one of the four requirements. This section runs the other
three on \textsc{FrJudge} \citep{beauchemin2025judgebert}, 297 pairs drawn from
two Quebec insurance forms, each simplified by \texttt{gpt-4-turbo-2024-04-09}
with a zero-shot prompt and rated by five law students on legal meaning
($1$--$10$), simplicity, and an 18-class legal characterization. It then asks
whether the two kinds of evidence agree.

\paragraph{Reconstruction.}
The public \texttt{FrJUDGE.jsonl} carries 1{,}485 rows ($297\times5$) but not
annotator identity, which makes Requirement 1 impossible for a downstream user;
the raw Prodigy export retains it. We rebuild the corpus from that export,
recovering the five annotators (pseudonymised A--E throughout; see
\S\ref{sec:ethics}) and the source document of each clause. Our mean pairwise
percent agreement is $25.96\%$, matching the published value exactly, and our
per-annotator means ($\annotMeans$) match to within rounding; everything
downstream rests on this check.

\subsection{Reliability and the structure of disagreement}
\label{sec:agreement}

Krippendorff's $\alpha$ for legal meaning is $0.104$ under the \emph{nominal}
coefficient, conventionally the one reported for this corpus, and $\alphaOrd$
under the \emph{ordinal} one (Table~\ref{tab:agreement},
Appendix~\ref{app:extra}). Legal meaning is measured on an ordered ten-point
scale and nominal $\alpha$ counts a one-point disagreement as fully as a
nine-point one, so the ordinal coefficient is the one appropriate to the design
\citep{krippendorff2004content,artstein2008inter}; the nominal values for
simplicity ($0.183$) and characterization ($0.552$) are genuinely nominal and
need no adjustment. The corpus is therefore more reliable than a nominal reading
suggests --- a point in its favour a reader would otherwise miss --- though
$\alphaOrd$ still indicates only fair agreement.

The disagreement that remains is structural rather than random
(Figure~\ref{fig:disagree}, Appendix~\ref{app:extra}). The five annotators
separate into two stable populations: B, C and D average $\lenientMean$ and A
and E average $\strictMean$, a gap of over three points on a ten-point scale.
Ratings are strongly bimodal (the modal values are $10$ and $1$), the median
across-annotator range on a single pair is $\medianRange$ points, and
\pctSpreadSix\ of pairs span at least six. Averaging produces a target with a
standard deviation of only $\labelSd$, much narrower than the underlying
ratings, which is precisely the condition under which a regressor that predicts
the middle scores well. The mean of five such ratings is a quantity no annotator
asserted, and this is why the two bounds below differ so much.

\subsection{The human ceiling}
\label{sec:ceiling}

Requirement 1 is directly executable here, because every item carries five
ratings. Correlating each annotator's ratings against the mean of the other four
(Table~\ref{tab:ceiling}, Appendix~\ref{app:extra}), the mean
leave-one-annotator-out correlation is $\ceilR$ (RMSE $\ceilRmse$); the
best-agreeing pair reaches $r=\bestPairR$ and the mean pairwise correlation is
$\meanPairR$.

A model exceeding $\ceilR$ is not thereby superhuman: predicting the \emph{mean}
of five raters is a different and much easier task than being one of them, and
one that rewards predicting the middle of a bimodal distribution. The
appropriate upper bound for predicting the observed mean is the reliability of
that mean, which by Spearman--Brown from the mean pairwise correlation is
$\sbRel$, so a metric can in principle approach $\sqrt{\sbRel}=\sbSqrt$.
Reporting both bounds separates two claims a single correlation runs together:
\emph{as good as an expert} ($r \approx \ceilR$) and \emph{as good as the
aggregate allows} ($r \approx \sbSqrt$). A correlation falling between them is
evidence for the second and evidence about nothing at all with respect to the
first. Any future legal-meaning metric should report against both.

\subsection{Metrics under identical supervision}
\label{sec:baselines}

Requirement 2 asks for a comparison in which every candidate sees the same
supervision, and Requirement 3 for a trivial control inside it. We take the
comparison set of \S\ref{sec:metriczoo} and add a supervised ridge regression
over ten surface features, mapping every metric's raw score to the human scale
by an isotonic regression fitted on the training split alone.

Three things in Table~\ref{tab:baselines} are worth separating. Calibration
collapses most of the RMSE gap that uncalibrated comparisons record: the metrics
were not as far from human judgment as such tables suggest, merely on a
different scale. The strongest single unsupervised signal is not a semantic one.
And a ridge regression over word counts and token overlap reaches $r=\surfR$,
at the human ceiling of \S\ref{sec:ceiling} and above every neural baseline;
whatever a fine-tuned legal metric adds beyond that, a comparison without this
row does not measure. We read this not as evidence that the corpus is trivial
but as evidence about what the \emph{annotation} rewards: score is reduced once
per identified error, the most frequent error type in LLM simplification is
omission, and omission is strongly correlated with becoming shorter. A metric
can therefore score well by detecting deletion, with no representation of legal
force. That hypothesis is what Requirement 4 exists to test.

\paragraph{Statistical adequacy.}
The test split holds $\testN$ items, at which size differences finer than
roughly a quarter of a correlation point are not resolvable, ours included. We
report bootstrap confidence intervals throughout
\citep{deutsch2021statistical} and, under the Williams test for dependent
correlations, the length feature's advantage is significant against every metric
in the comparison set (Appendix~\ref{app:williams}). This establishes that the
trivial baseline is genuinely stronger \emph{on this corpus}, not that the
neural metrics reason less well about law; distinguishing those two readings is
what the diagnostic is for.

\subsection{The two orderings come apart}
\label{sec:dissociation}

We can now put the two kinds of evidence side by side. Over the \nDissoc\ neural
baselines the rank correlation between calibrated $r$ on \textsc{FrJudge} and
\textsc{LegalEdit} margin is $\rho=\dissocRho$, with a $95\%$ bootstrap interval
of $[\dissocRhoLo, \dissocRhoHi]$: at this number of metrics, correlation with
the human label does not predict legal sensitivity in either direction. The
extremes show why it matters. The trivial length feature is the best-correlating
signal on the corpus ($r=0.641$) and has the lowest margin of anything we test
($\minMargin$, ranking the edited pair correctly only $11.3\%$ of the time),
while bidirectional NLI sits mid-table on correlation ($r=0.395$) and is the
only family that reacts to the edit at all. A metric selected on corpus
correlation alone would pick the wrong one. Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation} plots
the two axes against each other, and the emptiness of its upper-right quadrant
is the finding.

\subsection{Implications for training a metric}
\label{sec:objectives}

The protocol also constrains how one would fit a new metric.
Appendix~\ref{app:variants} reports seven CamemBERTv2 variants trained on
identical splits and evaluated on the human-annotated test split only, never on
a split containing their own augmentation. Three results bear on the protocol.
No training target moves the correlation by more than the scalar baseline's own
spread across seeds ($\pm0.082$); what the disagreement-aware objectives buy is
stability and calibration, the quantile head overshooting the human label on
$9.9\%$ of items against $25.4\%$ for the scalar head, so risk asymmetry can be
written into the loss rather than hoped for at no cost in correlation. Training
on a constraint buys satisfaction of it in distribution and nothing across a
change of source: every model, including the two whose mixture contains
identical pairs, scores $0\%$ on an identical-pair probe from a different
document, and \textsc{DA+LegalEdit}, trained on minimally-edited negatives,
reaches a margin of $0.051$ against $\maxMargin$ for untrained bidirectional
NLI. And scored on human items alone, the identical/unrelated augmentation costs
more than half the correlation on five seeds of five, because a figure computed
on a split containing that augmentation is dominated by identical pairs at $10$
and unrelated pairs at $1$ supplying most of the label variance. Separating the
two evaluations is Requirement 2 applied to training rather than to baselines.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Three checks, none of them a filter.}
A metric claiming to measure legal meaning should report three checks, not two:
identical pairs (does it saturate correctly?), unrelated pairs (does it bottom
out?), and \textsc{LegalEdit} (does it move when, and only when, the law moves?).
The first two are jointly satisfiable by lexical overlap and so cannot
distinguish a legal metric from a similarity function; the third cannot be
passed by overlap at all. That \textsc{LegalEdit} is passable matters as much as
that it is hard: because bidirectional NLI clears it while every similarity
metric does not, a low margin identifies a fixable deficiency rather than an
inherent limit of automatic evaluation.

The three should be reported \emph{jointly and separately}, and none used as an
admission criterion. Here the metric that scores highest on the diagnostic fails
the identical-pair check most badly, and a pipeline screening candidates on
saturation, as the current convention invites, would have discarded it before
the interesting question was asked. Saturation, bottoming out and legal
sensitivity are three independent properties; a scorer can be repaired for the
first two by calibration and cannot be repaired for the third at all. Rank on
the property that cannot be fixed. Nor has a metric trained on its own sanity
checks thereby passed them: our augmented models pass the unrelated check on
$77\%$ and $69\%$ of probes and the identical check on none, and the only
difference is whether the probe comes from the same documents as the training
pairs (\S\ref{sec:objectives}). The honest report separates ``satisfies the
constraint by construction'' from ``generalises to the constraint''.

\paragraph{Entailment is the better starting point.}
The construct \textsc{FrJudge} annotates is defined by an error taxonomy whose
two dominant categories are omission and hallucination --- precisely the two
directions of entailment failure --- and the only scorer that reacts to a
legally decisive edit is the one built from bidirectional entailment. We would
not conclude that an off-the-shelf multilingual NLI model is an adequate legal
metric: its correlation with the human label is middling ($r=0.395$), it is
slower than a fine-tuned encoder, and its margin of $\maxMargin$ leaves a third
of the range unspent. The narrower conclusion is that the next legal-meaning
metric should be built on an entailment objective and evaluated on the
diagnostic, rather than on a similarity objective selected by corpus
correlation; a supervised model trained against \textsc{FrJudge} \emph{and}
minimally-edited negatives is the obvious candidate.

\paragraph{Disagreement is the finding, not the noise.}
That two of five Quebec law students systematically rate legal preservation
three points lower than the other three is not an annotation defect to be
averaged away. It measures how much legal interpretation of an isolated clause
varies among trained readers, and any deployed metric returning a single number
conceals it. A metric that reports ``6.2, experts ranged 2--9'' tells a
practitioner something actionable --- this clause needs a human --- which
``6.2'' does not.

\section{Conclusion}

Legal meaning preservation is the right construct to evaluate, and the resources
that now exist for it deserve to be built on. But the evidence a metric paper in
this area conventionally offers --- a correlation with an averaged human label,
computed against uncalibrated baselines, without a trivial control, on sanity
checks the model may have been trained on --- cannot establish that the metric
measures legal meaning rather than lexical overlap and length. The missing
ingredient is a dissociation, and it is cheap: hold surface form fixed and move
the law. On that test every similarity-based metric we examined scores a
sentence saying the opposite of the original as though nothing had changed, and
reversing which party a clause binds is among the least detectable edits of all.
The test is not unpassable --- bidirectional entailment, a family the standard
comparison leaves out and the standard sanity check would have disqualified,
clears most of it --- which points at what the next version of such a metric
should be made of. It also carries a warning about how these metrics are
selected, because the signal that ranks best on the corpus ranks last on the law.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

Our \textsc{LegalEdit} labels are definitional rather than annotated: we assert
that changing \emph{doit} to \emph{peut} changes legal meaning, rather than
having lawyers rate each perturbation. We think this is defensible for tier-1
edits, which alter modality, party, polarity or quantum, and we report tier-2
scope shifts separately for exactly this reason; but a human-validated version
would be stronger, and a small expert study is the obvious next step. The
perturbations are also single-edit and template-generated, so they probe a
specific and narrow kind of legal change; a system could in principle be tuned
to them without acquiring general legal sensitivity, which is why we treat
\textsc{LegalEdit} as a necessary condition and not a benchmark to be optimised.

That caveat bears directly on our most encouraging result, and we would rather
state it than let a reader find it. Our perturbations are largely negation,
modality, quantifier and antonym contrasts, which is close to the inventory
natural language inference training data is built around. A model fine-tuned on
XNLI has seen a great many \emph{ne \dots pas} insertions and
\emph{tous}/\emph{certains} alternations, so bidirectional NLI's margin of
$\maxMargin$ may reflect familiarity with the \emph{form} of our edits rather
than sensitivity to their legal consequence. The two hypotheses make different
predictions: only the second should survive perturbations that change legal
force without changing logical form, such as substituting a defined term for a
near-synonym that is not its legal equivalent. Separating them needs a version
of the diagnostic built around defined-term substitution, which our rule
inventory reaches only through the single \emph{automobile}/\emph{v\'ehicule}
case. We report the NLI result as a promising direction with an identified
confound, not as evidence that entailment models read law.

A further mismatch is that the diagnostic and the correlation evaluation run on
different text. \textsc{FrJudge} is drawn from insurance forms, while the
\textsc{LegalEdit} probes are generated from two Quebec statutes, because the
statutes supply the volume and the regularity of phrasing that the perturbation
rules need. The two are the same language and register but not the same genre,
so Table~\ref{tab:challenge} and Table~\ref{tab:baselines} answer different
questions about the same metric and should not be read as one ranking.

We do not evaluate an LLM-as-judge baseline, and this is the most significant
gap in our comparison. Recent legal evaluation work \citep{zhou2024lemaj}
suggests that a prompted frontier model given the annotators' own rubric is the
natural competitor to a fine-tuned 112M-parameter encoder, and a demonstration
that the small supervised model matches it would be a strong efficiency result.
We note two reasons the omission does not affect our conclusions. Our claims are
about what a corpus-correlation comparison establishes, and about a diagnostic
property, sensitivity to a legally decisive edit, that is a necessary condition
for any scorer, prompted or fine-tuned; and our evaluation harness takes an
arbitrary scoring function, so \textsc{LegalEdit} can be run against a prompted
judge without modification. We release the harness with that interface so the
comparison can be completed.

Like the corpus we build on, we evaluate isolated clauses out of document
context, and all simplifications come from a single generator
(\texttt{gpt-4-turbo}) with one prompt. A metric meta-evaluation properly
requires outputs from several systems spanning a range of quality; with one
generator, a metric may be learning that generator's characteristic failure
modes. Appendix~\ref{app:grouped} addresses document-level leakage but cannot
address generator-level overfitting.

Finally, our human ceiling is computed on all 297 items while the model
correlations are computed on $\testN$-item test splits, and the ceiling carries
its own sampling uncertainty (per-annotator 95\% CIs in
Table~\ref{tab:ceiling}). The comparison ``model $>$ ceiling'' should be read
with those intervals in view.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:ethics}

\paragraph{Annotator privacy.}
\textsc{FrJudge}'s five annotators are pseudonymised as A--E in the paper that
introduces them, and we use those pseudonyms throughout. Our released code
pseudonymises annotator identifiers on load, and no annotator name appears in
any result file, table or figure we release. We also ship the pseudonymisation
script separately so that anyone redistributing a reconstruction of the corpus
can apply it before doing so.

\paragraph{Use of the metric.}
Our results strengthen a caution \citet{beauchemin2025judgebert} themselves
raise. A metric that cannot distinguish \emph{doit} from \emph{peut} must not be
used as an automated gate on whether a simplified clause is safe to publish. We
regard the appropriate deployment as triage, that is, the ranking of clauses for
human review and the flagging of disagreement, rather than as certification.
Nothing here constitutes legal advice.

\paragraph{Source material.}
The statutes from which \textsc{LegalEdit} is generated are public legal texts.
The perturbed sentences are deliberate misstatements of Quebec law and are
released as an evaluation artefact only; they are labelled as such in the
distribution and should not be quoted as statements of the law.

\section{Training Variants in Full}
\label{app:variants}

The protocol so far audits scorers that already exist. It also has implications
for how one would fit a new one, and this section tests two of them. The first
follows from \S\ref{sec:agreement}: the gold label is the mean of five ratings
whose median spread is \medianRange\ points, so fitting a scalar regressor to
that mean asks the model to predict a quantity none of the annotators asserted,
and rewards predicting the centre. The second follows from
\S\ref{sec:protocol}: if a diagnostic identifies a deficiency, the obvious
response is to train against it.

The seven configurations share an encoder (CamemBERTv2-base), splits, schedule
and seeds, and differ only in the target and the loss.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Scalar} (the conventional objective): MSE against the mean rating.
\item \textbf{Distributional}: a ten-way head trained with KL divergence against
      the empirical rating histogram, following \citet{uma2021learning} and
      \citet{peterson2019human}. The reported score is the distribution's
      expectation, so it is directly comparable, but the model additionally
      exposes its spread.
\item \textbf{Annotator-aware}: five regression heads, one per annotator,
      averaged at inference. This lets the model represent the lenient and
      strict populations of \S\ref{sec:agreement} separately instead of
      averaging them away.
\item \textbf{Quantile}: a pinball loss \citep{koenker1978regression} at
      $\tau=0.25$. The asymmetry is deliberate. A metric that
      \emph{overshoots} the human rating is permissive of bad simplifications,
      while one that undershoots is merely strict, and in a legal setting those
      errors are not symmetric; a quantile objective encodes that preference in
      the loss rather than hoping it emerges.
\item \textbf{Multi-task}: the scalar head plus an auxiliary characterization
      head, trained against the majority vote of the five annotators, which
      spans 17 of the rubric's 18 classes (the eighteenth never carries a
      majority). The characterization labels are collected in \textsc{FrJudge}
      and then discarded; since the published rubric has annotators characterize
      a clause \emph{before} scoring it, the label plausibly carries information
      about the score.
\item \textbf{DA}: the scalar objective with the identical/unrelated
      augmentation in current use added to the training mixture, unchanged.
\item \textbf{DA+LegalEdit}: \textbf{DA} plus minimally-edited negatives
      generated from \textsc{FrJudge} originals by the rules of
      \S\ref{sec:legaledit}, disjoint from the diagnostic's own sentences.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.} Correlation and RMSE are computed \emph{only}
on the human-annotated test split, never on a split containing augmentation.
This matters: adding identical pairs (label 10) and unrelated pairs (label 1) to
the evaluation set inflates label variance at the extremes, which raises Pearson
$r$ for every metric without any improvement in judgment. A table that does not
separate the two shows the signature clearly, in that a metric's $r$ and its
RMSE improve and worsen together rather than moving as one. Augmentation here
affects training only; the three diagnostic regimes are reported separately as
pass rates.

\paragraph{Disagreement-aware objectives buy stability, not accuracy.}
Table~\ref{tab:models} reports the grid, and the first result is a negative one:
no training target moves the correlation by more than the scalar baseline's own
spread across seeds ($\pm0.082$), which is itself smaller than the
$\mindetect$ gap \S\ref{sec:baselines} showed to be resolvable on an
$\testN$-item split. The differences that survive are in the other columns. The
distributional head is markedly steadier than the scalar head it replaces
($\pm0.029$ against $\pm0.082$ over the same five seeds), reaches the best RMSE
in the table ($1.99$), and roughly doubles the rank correlation ($\rho=0.578$
against $0.280$), which is what one expects from a target carrying the shape of
the rating distribution and not only its mean. The quantile head does precisely
what it was asked to do: it overshoots the human label on $9.9\%$ of test items
against $25.4\%$ for the scalar head, the lowest rate of any model here. The
risk asymmetry a legal setting calls for can therefore be written into the loss
rather than hoped for, and it costs nothing in correlation.

\paragraph{Trained constraints do not cross a change of document.}
Every model in Table~\ref{tab:models} scores $0\%$ on the identical-pair check,
the two augmented ones included, although identical pairs are in their training
mixture; the augmented models pass the unrelated check on $77\%$ and $69\%$ of
probes and the un-augmented models on none. The asymmetry is the one our probe
design predicts. Our unrelated probes are drawn from the same two statutes the
training mixture draws its unrelated pairs from, so passing that check is an
in-distribution result; our identical probes are statutory sentences while the
mixture's identical pairs are \textsc{FrJudge} clauses, so passing that one
would require generalising across source documents, and no model does. The same
holds for the diagnostic: \textsc{DA+LegalEdit}, trained on minimally-edited
negatives, reaches a margin of $0.051$ against $\maxMargin$ for untrained
bidirectional NLI, its negatives generated from \textsc{FrJudge} originals and
its probes from statutes. Training on a constraint buys satisfaction of it in
distribution and nothing at all across a change of source.

The same separation explains the augmented mixtures' correlations, which are the
clearest effect in the table: \textsc{DA} loses $0.350$ against the un-augmented
baseline and \textsc{DA+LegalEdit} $0.420$, on five seeds out of five in both
cases, and \textsc{DA} varies by $\pm0.254$ across seeds, reaching $r=-0.188$ on
one of them.

One column of Table~\ref{tab:models} should be read with care.
\textsc{LegalEdit} false-acceptance is the share of flipped pairs still scored
$\geq 7$, so it is confounded with where a model puts its predictions overall.
The distributional head's $69\%$ reflects a head that scores high in general
(its identical probes average $7.22$), and the $0\%$ of the scalar heads
reflects models that score low on everything, not models that reject the edit.

\section{What Survives a Change of Document}
\label{app:grouped}

A metric meta-evaluation that never changes source document cannot distinguish
legal sensitivity from familiarity with a form. We therefore add an
out-of-domain split. \textsc{FrJudge} clauses come from identifiable source
documents, which we recover from the raw export. Random splitting lets clauses
from the same insurance form appear in both training and test, and insurance
forms are highly templated. We compare the standard random split against a
document-grouped split in which no source document straddles the boundary
(Table~\ref{tab:models_grouped}); the difference estimates how much of the
reported performance is form-specific. We rerun three of the seven
configurations of Table~\ref{tab:models} on that split: the scalar baseline and
the two augmented mixtures. The remaining four differ from the baseline in what
they are trained to predict rather than in what they can exploit about a source
form, so the leakage they are exposed to is the same.

The corpus makes that harder than it sounds. Its 297 pairs come from only 21
source forms and the three largest hold 71\% of them, so the obvious
construction, sweeping the forms in random order and cutting at the cumulative
60/70\% marks, lets a single large form overshoot a boundary and starve the
split behind it. On seeds 42--46 that procedure returns test splits of 16, 87,
30, 0 and 88 pairs, one of them empty and one far too small to carry a
correlation. We therefore choose the form-to-split assignment by a short local
search over total deviation from the target sizes. Thousands of assignments hit
$178/30/89$ exactly, so each seed still selects a genuinely different partition
while every split matches the random-split sizes of Table~\ref{tab:models} pair
for pair, and the two tables differ only in which forms are held out. One
limitation is structural rather than incidental: the largest form is 37\% of the
corpus, larger than the 30\% test quota, so it always falls in training and the
grouped test set is always drawn from the remaining 188 pairs.

\paragraph{The comparison does not resolve.} The scalar baseline's correlation
falls from $0.542$ on the random split to $0.454$ on the grouped one, a drop of
$0.088$ that is in the direction leakage would predict. It does not survive
inspection. The per-seed differences are $+0.169$, $-0.097$, $-0.416$, $-0.182$
and $+0.086$: two of the five seeds \emph{improve} under the harder split, and
the grouped spread ($\pm0.143$) is wider than the gap itself. The two augmented
mixtures move by $-0.002$ and $+0.026$, which is to say not at all. As a
control, the untrained NLI reference is fitted to nothing and should be
indifferent to how the corpus is partitioned; it moves from $0.395$ to $0.391$,
confirming that the two splits are otherwise comparable and that the variance
above is the models' and not the split's.

We therefore do not claim that document-level leakage inflates the correlations
of \S\ref{sec:objectives}, and we hold our own evidence to the standard
\S\ref{sec:baselines} applies elsewhere. What we can say is narrower and, we
think, more useful to anyone planning a successor corpus: \textsc{FrJudge} is
too small and too concentrated to answer the question. Its grouped test split is
always drawn from the $188$ pairs the largest form leaves behind, at which size
the seed-to-seed variation swamps the effect being measured. A corpus intended
to support an out-of-domain claim needs its documents spread far more evenly
than three forms holding $71\%$ of the data, and this is the aspect of the
design we would change first.

\section{Stratifying by Length Change}
\label{app:buckets}

We further break the test predictions down by the magnitude of the length change
between original and simplification. If a metric's correlation is concentrated
in the buckets where the simplification is much shorter, it is detecting
deletion rather than legal change, the hypothesis raised in
\S\ref{sec:baselines}. Table~\ref{tab:buckets} reports Pearson $r$ inside each
tercile of $|\,|s|-|o|\,|$. Within a tercile the length signal is largely held
constant, so a metric that retains its correlation there is using something
other than deletion; one whose correlation collapses was reading length all
along. This is a weaker form of the same dissociation logic as
\S\ref{sec:protocol}: it holds a confound approximately fixed by conditioning
rather than exactly fixed by construction.

The result qualifies \S\ref{sec:baselines} in a way that is worth stating
carefully, because it cuts against our own argument. The length feature's
correlation is overwhelmingly a \emph{between}-tercile effect: pooled over the
corpus it reaches $0.613$, but inside the small and medium terciles it falls to
$0.243$ and $0.165$. Once items of comparable length change are compared with
each other, knowing the length change tells you little. It recovers to $0.585$
only in the tercile where the simplification is much shorter than the original,
which is exactly where deletion is available to be detected. The neural
baselines have the opposite profile: in the medium tercile \textsc{BERTScore}
over FlauBERT reaches $0.449$ and LaBSE $0.532$, at or above their pooled values
of $0.484$ and $0.424$, where the length feature retains almost nothing. The
supervised surface ridge tracks the raw length feature bucket for bucket, which
is what one would expect if it is largely a length model with extra terms. The
small tercile is hard for everything, which we read as a floor effect rather
than as a result: when the two sentences are close to the same length, none of
these signals has much to go on.

The honest reading is therefore narrower than ``legal meaning preservation is
length preservation''. The corpus-level ranking is dominated by length, and any
comparison that omits a length control will attribute to a metric what it should
attribute to that confound. But the neural metrics are not \emph{only} reading
length. Where the length signal is weakest, they are the ones that hold up,
keeping or exceeding the correlation they had over the whole corpus; whatever
they are reading in that stratum, it is not the length change. What
\S\ref{sec:results} adds is that the residual signal they retain is still not
legal sensitivity, since the same metrics move almost not at all when the law is
changed and the length is held exactly fixed. The two analyses answer different
questions, and a metric has to survive both.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}

Every number in this paper is produced by a single pipeline that runs end to end
on a laptop; the largest model involved is CamemBERTv2-base (112M parameters)
and no GPU cluster is required. We release the experiment code, the
reconstruction of \textsc{FrJudge} from the raw export (which recovers annotator
identity and source document, both dropped from the public JSONL), the
document-grouped splits, the \textsc{LegalEdit} generator and scoring harness,
and the result files behind every table.

One caveat on exact reproduction: our training runs use Apple MPS kernels, which
are not bitwise deterministic, so a fixed seed reproduces a run only
approximately: repeat runs of the scalar configuration at seed $42$ differ in
test-set $r$ by several hundredths, which is comparable to the spread across
seeds. We therefore report means and standard deviations over five seeds and
treat any difference smaller than that spread as unresolved, the same discipline
\S\ref{sec:baselines} asks of everyone else, applied to ourselves. Anyone
reproducing this on CUDA should expect tighter agreement and correspondingly
narrower error bars.

Two further details matter for anyone reproducing the diagnostic. The first is
what the probes share with training, which differs by regime and which we state
precisely because conflating the two is one of the failure modes the protocol is
meant to prevent. The \emph{identical} probe is built from statutory sentences
(the Quebec Automobile Insurance Act and the Highway Safety Code) while the
identical pairs in the \textsc{-DA} training mixture are built from
\textsc{FrJudge}'s insurance-form clauses, so a model passes that probe only by
generalising across source documents. The \textsc{LegalEdit} probe is likewise
generated from statutory sentences, while the minimally-edited negatives added
by the \textsc{DA+LegalEdit} variant are generated from \textsc{FrJudge}
originals: same perturbation rules, disjoint sentences. The \emph{unrelated}
probe is the weakest of the three in this respect: both it and the unrelated
pairs in the training mixture are drawn from the same two statutes, under
different random seeds, so a high unrelated pass rate for a \textsc{-DA} model
should be read as in-distribution and we do not treat it as evidence of
generalisation. Second, the ordinal Krippendorff coefficient we report is
computed with the same library and the same reliability matrix as the nominal
one, so the difference between $0.104$ and $\alphaOrd$ is attributable to the
distance function alone.
